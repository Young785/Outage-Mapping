## COURSE INFORMATION & OBJECTIVES

### Course Description
Anatomy and Physiology I (CHE 212) is a foundational course designed to provide Community Health Extension Workers with a thorough understanding of the normal structure and function of the human body. The course begins at the cellular and chemical level, progresses through tissues and glandular systems, and culminates in detailed coverage of the blood, cardiovascular, and lymphatic systems. Emphasis is placed on clinical correlations, community health applications, and practical skills necessary for disease prevention, health promotion, and primary health care delivery.

### General Course Objectives
By the end of this course, students should be able to:

1. Define anatomy and physiology and explain their interrelationship in maintaining human health.
2. Describe the levels of organization of the human body from atoms to organ systems.
3. Explain homeostasis, identify the major components of feedback systems, and relate homeostatic imbalance to common community health conditions.
4. Describe the composition, distribution, and functions of body fluids and electrolytes, and recognize the clinical signs of fluid and electrolyte imbalances.
5. Identify cell structures and explain their functions, including the processes of cell division, protein synthesis, and energy production.
6. Classify the four main types of tissues, describe their structures and functions, and relate tissue damage to common injuries and diseases.
7. Differentiate between exocrine and endocrine glands, describe the major endocrine organs, and explain the clinical manifestations of hormonal imbalances.
8. Describe the composition and functions of blood, explain blood grouping and clotting mechanisms, and identify common hematological disorders relevant to community practice.
9. Explain the anatomy and physiology of the heart and blood vessels, describe the cardiac cycle, blood pressure regulation, and identify emergency cardiovascular signs.
10. Describe the lymphatic system and immune response, differentiate between innate and adaptive immunity, and explain the importance of vaccination in community health.

### Learning Outcomes
Upon successful completion of this course, the student will be able to:
- Use correct anatomical terminology to describe body positions, directions, planes, and cavities.
- Explain the physiological mechanisms underlying temperature regulation, blood glucose control, and acid-base balance.
- Identify the structural components of cells, tissues, and organs and relate structure to function.
- Perform basic assessments related to blood pressure, pulse, dehydration, and anemia in a community setting.
- Educate community members on the prevention and early recognition of cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, infections, and cancer.
- Apply knowledge of immunity and vaccination to community health programs.

## MODULE 1: INTRODUCTION TO THE HUMAN BODY & HOMEOSTASIS

### Overview
This module introduces the foundational concepts of anatomy and physiology, establishes the organizational hierarchy of the human body, and explains the critical principle of homeostasis. Understanding these basics is essential for CHEWS because every clinical condition can ultimately be traced to a structural abnormality or a physiological disruption.

### 1.1 Definition of Anatomy and Physiology

**Anatomy** is the branch of science that deals with the study of the structure of the body and the physical relationships between its parts. The word "anatomy" is derived from the Greek words "ana" (up) and "tome" (cutting), literally meaning "to cut up." Historically, anatomical knowledge was gained through dissection. In community health, anatomy helps the CHEW understand where organs are located, how they are arranged, and what structural changes occur during disease.

**Physiology** is the study of how the body and its parts function or work. It is derived from the Greek word "physis" (nature) and "logos" (study). Physiology focuses on the chemical and physical processes that keep the body alive and functioning—such as how the heart pumps blood, how the lungs exchange gases, and how the kidneys filter waste.

**The Relationship Between Anatomy and Physiology:**  
Anatomy and physiology are inseparable. Structure determines function, and function influences structure. For example:
- The anatomy of the red blood cell (biconcave disc shape, no nucleus) maximizes its surface area for oxygen transport—this is structure supporting function.
- Regular physical exercise (a physiological activity) leads to enlargement of the heart muscle (structural change).

A CHEW who understands both can better appreciate why a diseased heart valve (anatomical defect) causes breathlessness (physiological consequence), or why prolonged high blood sugar (physiological) damages blood vessels (anatomical).

### 1.2 Branches of Anatomy and Physiology

**Branches of Anatomy:**
- **Gross (Macroscopic) Anatomy:** Study of structures visible to the naked eye. Examples include the study of muscles, bones, and organs during surgery or post-mortem.
- **Microscopic Anatomy:** Study of structures too small to be seen without magnification. Includes histology (study of tissues) and cytology (study of cells).
- **Developmental Anatomy:** Study of structural changes from conception to adulthood. Embryology is a subspecialty focusing on the first eight weeks of development.
- **Regional Anatomy:** Study of specific regions of the body (e.g., the head and neck, or the thorax).
- **Systemic Anatomy:** Study of the body by organ systems (e.g., skeletal system, muscular system, cardiovascular system).
- **Surface Anatomy:** Study of internal structures as they relate to the overlying skin surface. Very important for CHEWS when locating pulse points, injection sites, or landmarks for physical examination.
- **Radiographic Anatomy:** Study of body structures using imaging techniques such as X-rays, CT scans, and MRI.
- **Pathological Anatomy:** Study of structural changes associated with disease.

**Branches of Physiology:**
- **Cell Physiology:** Study of cellular functions such as metabolism, transport, and signaling.
- **Neurophysiology:** Study of the functioning of the nervous system.
- **Endocrinology:** Study of hormones and glandular function.
- **Cardiovascular Physiology:** Study of the heart and blood vessels.
- **Respiratory Physiology:** Study of lung function and gas exchange.
- **Renal Physiology:** Study of kidney function and urine formation.
- **Exercise Physiology:** Study of body changes during physical activity.
- **Pathophysiology:** Study of functional changes associated with disease and injury. This is particularly relevant for CHEWS, as it explains the mechanisms behind symptoms.

### 1.3 Levels of Organization of the Human Body

The human body is organized in a precise hierarchy. Understanding these levels helps CHEWS appreciate how a problem at one level affects all others.

**Level 1: Chemical Level**  
The simplest level. Atoms (e.g., carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen) combine to form molecules. Examples include water (H₂O), glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆), DNA, and proteins. The chemical level is the foundation of all body structures and functions.

**Level 2: Cellular Level**  
Molecules combine to form cells—the basic structural and functional units of life. Examples include muscle cells, nerve cells, and epithelial cells. Each cell type has specialized structures that enable specific functions.

**Level 3: Tissue Level**  
Groups of similar cells and their intercellular substances work together to perform a specific function. The four primary tissue types are epithelial, connective, muscle, and nervous tissue.

**Level 4: Organ Level**  
Different tissue types combine to form organs, each with a distinct structure and function. Examples include the heart (muscle, connective, epithelial, and nervous tissues), the liver, and the skin.

**Level 5: System Level**  
Organs that work together to perform a common function constitute an organ system. Examples include the cardiovascular system (heart, blood vessels, blood), the digestive system, and the respiratory system.

**Level 6: Organismal Level**  
All organ systems functioning together make up the living human being—the total organism.

**Clinical Relevance for CHEWS:**  
When a patient presents with leg swelling, a CHEWS should think across levels:
- Chemical: Are plasma proteins low?
- Cellular: Are kidney cells damaged?
- Tissue: Is there inflammation in connective tissue?
- Organ: Is the heart failing?
- System: Is venous return impaired?
- Organism: Is the whole body fluid balance disrupted?

### 1.4 Anatomical Position and Directional Terms

**Anatomical Position:**  
The standard reference position used in anatomy to avoid confusion. In anatomical position, the person stands erect, facing forward, arms at the sides with palms facing forward, and feet slightly apart with toes pointing forward.

**Directional Terms:**

| Term | Definition | Example |
|------|------------|---------|
| **Superior (Cranial)** | Toward the head or upper part | The head is superior to the chest |
| **Inferior (Caudal)** | Away from the head, toward the feet | The stomach is inferior to the lungs |
| **Anterior (Ventral)** | Toward the front of the body | The ribs are anterior to the lungs |
| **Posterior (Dorsal)** | Toward the back of the body | The spine is posterior to the heart |
| **Medial** | Toward the midline of the body | The nose is medial to the eyes |
| **Lateral** | Away from the midline | The ears are lateral to the nose |
| **Proximal** | Closer to the point of attachment or origin | The elbow is proximal to the wrist |
| **Distal** | Farther from the point of attachment | The fingers are distal to the wrist |
| **Superficial (External)** | Close to the body surface | The skin is superficial to muscles |
| **Deep (Internal)** | Farther from the body surface | Bones are deep to the skin |
| **Ipsilateral** | On the same side of the body | The right hand and right foot are ipsilateral |
| **Contralateral** | On opposite sides of the body | The left hand and right foot are contralateral |

**Clinical Application:**  
CHEWS must use these terms accurately when referring patients, documenting wounds, or describing the location of lumps, rashes, or pain. For example, a wound on the "anterior aspect of the proximal left forearm" communicates precise location to any health professional.

### 1.5 Body Planes and Sections

**Sagittal Plane:** Divides the body into right and left parts. A midsagittal (median) plane divides it into equal right and left halves.

**Frontal (Coronal) Plane:** Divides the body into anterior (front) and posterior (back) portions.

**Transverse (Horizontal) Plane:** Divides the body into superior (upper) and inferior (lower) portions. Also called a cross-section.

**Oblique Plane:** Any plane that is not sagittal, frontal, or transverse; cuts through the body at an angle.

**Clinical Application:**  
Imaging techniques such as CT and MRI produce images in these planes. A CHEWS who understands planes can better interpret radiology reports and explain findings to patients.

### 1.6 Body Cavities

Body cavities are spaces within the body that contain and protect internal organs.

**Dorsal Cavity (Posterior):**
- **Cranial Cavity:** Contains the brain, protected by the skull.
- **Spinal (Vertebral) Cavity:** Contains the spinal cord, protected by the vertebrae.

**Ventral Cavity (Anterior):**  
The ventral cavity is separated into the thoracic cavity and the abdominopelvic cavity by the diaphragm.

- **Thoracic Cavity:** Contains the heart and lungs.
  - **Mediastinum:** Central compartment containing the heart, trachea, esophagus, and major blood vessels.
  - **Pleural Cavities:** Each contains a lung.
  - **Pericardial Cavity:** Contains the heart.

- **Abdominopelvic Cavity:** Divided into:
  - **Abdominal Cavity:** Contains the stomach, liver, gallbladder, pancreas, spleen, kidneys, small intestine, and most of the large intestine.
  - **Pelvic Cavity:** Contains the urinary bladder, reproductive organs, and the terminal part of the large intestine.

**Other Cavities:**
- **Oral Cavity:** Mouth.
- **Nasal Cavity:** Within the nose.
- **Orbital Cavities:** Eye sockets.
- **Synovial Cavities:** Within joints.

### 1.7 Homeostasis and Feedback Mechanisms

**Definition of Homeostasis:**  
Homeostasis is the maintenance of a relatively stable internal environment despite continuous changes in the external environment. The term comes from Greek words meaning "staying the same." It is the most critical concept in physiology. Every organ system contributes to homeostasis.

**Components of a Homeostatic Control System:**
1. **Receptor (Sensor):** Detects changes in the internal environment and sends information to the control center. Example: Temperature receptors in the skin and hypothalamus.
2. **Control Center:** Processes the information and determines the appropriate response. Usually the brain or spinal cord. Example: The hypothalamus regulates body temperature.
3. **Effector:** The structure that carries out the response. Example: Sweat glands, blood vessels, or muscles.

**Feedback Mechanisms:**

**Negative Feedback:**  
The most common type of feedback in the body. In negative feedback, the output of a system reduces or shuts off the original stimulus, thereby reversing the condition and restoring homeostasis.
- **Mechanism:** Stimulus → Receptor → Control Center → Effector → Response that reduces the stimulus.
- **Example: Body Temperature Regulation**
  - When body temperature rises above 37°C (stimulus), temperature receptors in the skin and hypothalamus detect it.
  - The hypothalamus (control center) sends signals to effectors.
  - Sweat glands produce sweat; blood vessels in the skin dilate (vasodilation) to release heat.
  - Body temperature falls back toward normal.
- **Example: Blood Glucose Regulation**
  - After a meal, blood glucose rises.
  - The pancreas detects this and releases insulin.
  - Insulin causes cells to absorb glucose and the liver to store it as glycogen.
  - Blood glucose returns to normal.
  - If blood glucose falls too low, the pancreas releases glucagon, which stimulates the liver to convert glycogen back to glucose, raising blood sugar.
- **Other Examples:** Blood pressure regulation (baroreceptor reflex), oxygen and carbon dioxide levels (chemoreceptor reflex), and calcium levels (parathyroid hormone).

**Positive Feedback:**  
In positive feedback, the output enhances or amplifies the original stimulus, pushing the system further away from its initial state. Positive feedback is less common and usually drives processes to completion rather than maintaining stability.
- **Mechanism:** Stimulus → Response → Greater stimulus → Greater response.
- **Example: Blood Clotting**
  - A vessel is injured, and platelets release clotting factors.
  - These factors attract more platelets, which release more factors.
  - The cascade continues until a clot forms and seals the vessel.
- **Example: Childbirth (Uterine Contractions)**
  - Oxytocin stimulates uterine contractions.
  - Contractions push the baby against the cervix.
  - Stretching of the cervix signals the hypothalamus to release more oxytocin.
  - Contractions become stronger and more frequent until delivery.
- **Example: Action Potential Generation in Nerve Cells**
  - Sodium influx triggers more sodium channels to open.

**Comparison of Feedback Mechanisms:**

| Feature | Negative Feedback | Positive Feedback |
|---------|-------------------|-------------------|
| **Goal** | Maintain stability, reverse change | Amplify change, drive process to completion |
| **Response to stimulus** | Opposes the stimulus | Reinforces the stimulus |
| **Result** | Returns variable to set point | Moves variable away from set point |
| **Frequency** | Very common | Rare |
| **Examples** | Temperature, blood glucose, blood pressure | Blood clotting, childbirth, nerve impulses |

### 1.8 Examples of Homeostatic Imbalance

Homeostatic imbalance occurs when the body's control mechanisms cannot maintain stability, leading to disease.

**Fever:**
- Fever is an abnormal increase in body temperature, usually in response to infection.
- The hypothalamic set point is raised by pyrogens (fever-producing substances released by pathogens or immune cells).
- The body then acts as if its normal temperature is too low, initiating heat-conserving and heat-generating mechanisms: shivering, vasoconstriction, and increased metabolism.
- Clinical relevance: CHEWS must recognize that moderate fever can be protective (enhancing immune function and inhibiting bacterial growth), but very high fever (>40°C) can cause brain damage, seizures, and death—especially in children. Management includes tepid sponging, hydration, and antipyretics.

**Diabetes Mellitus:**
- A classic example of homeostatic failure in blood glucose regulation.
- Type 1: The pancreas fails to produce insulin (autoimmune destruction of beta cells).
- Type 2: Body cells become resistant to insulin, and the pancreas cannot produce enough to overcome this resistance.
- Result: Chronic hyperglycemia (high blood glucose), which damages blood vessels, nerves, kidneys, and eyes.
- CHEWS role: Health education on diet, exercise, early detection, medication adherence, and foot care.

**Dehydration:**
- Occurs when fluid loss exceeds fluid intake, disrupting fluid and electrolyte homeostasis.
- Causes: Diarrhea, vomiting, excessive sweating, burns, or inadequate water intake.
- Signs: Thirst, dry mucous membranes, reduced urine output, sunken eyes, decreased skin turgor, tachycardia, hypotension.
- Severe dehydration leads to hypovolemic shock, kidney failure, and death.
- CHEWS role: Oral rehydration therapy (ORT) education, promoting breastfeeding, encouraging fluid intake during hot weather, and recognizing danger signs.

**Hypertension (High Blood Pressure):**
- Normal blood pressure is approximately 120/80 mmHg. Persistent readings above 140/90 mmHg indicate hypertension.
- It results from failure of the baroreceptor reflex and other blood pressure regulatory mechanisms.
- Causes include excessive salt intake, obesity, stress, kidney disease, and genetic factors.
- It is often called the "silent killer" because it damages arteries, the heart, brain, and kidneys without obvious symptoms.
- CHEWS role: Blood pressure screening, lifestyle counseling (reduced salt, exercise, weight management), adherence support for antihypertensive medications, and referral.

## MODULE 2: BODY FLUIDS AND BASIC CHEMISTRY

### Overview
The human body is approximately 60% water. Body fluids serve as the medium for transport, chemical reactions, temperature regulation, and cellular function. CHEWS frequently encounter conditions where fluid and electrolyte balance is disrupted—diarrhea, vomiting, burns, and heat exhaustion. This module provides the scientific foundation for understanding, preventing, and managing these conditions.

### 2.1 Total Body Water

Water is the most abundant substance in the body. In a 70 kg adult male, total body water (TBW) is approximately 42 liters (about 60% of body weight). In females, it is about 50% due to higher adipose tissue, which contains less water. In infants, TBW may be as high as 75%, whereas in elderly people, it declines to about 45-50%.

**Distribution of Total Body Water:**
- **Intracellular Fluid (ICF):** Fluid inside the cells. Constitutes about two-thirds (28 liters) of TBW. Rich in potassium (K⁺), magnesium (Mg²⁺), and phosphate ions.
- **Extracellular Fluid (ECF):** Fluid outside the cells. Constitutes about one-third (14 liters) of TBW. Rich in sodium (Na⁺), chloride (Cl⁻), and bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻).
  - **Interstitial Fluid (IF):** Fluid in the spaces between cells (about 11 liters).
  - **Plasma:** The fluid component of blood (about 3 liters).
  - **Transcellular Fluid:** Small volumes of specialized fluids such as cerebrospinal fluid, synovial fluid, pleural fluid, peritoneal fluid, and lymph.

**Importance of Body Water:**
- It is the solvent in which metabolic reactions occur.
- It transports nutrients, hormones, and waste products.
- It regulates body temperature through sweating and evaporation.
- It provides lubrication for joints, mucous membranes, and organs.
- It acts as a cushion for sensitive tissues such as the brain and spinal cord.

### 2.2 Intracellular and Extracellular Fluids

**Composition Differences:**
The composition of ICF and ECF is very different, and this is critical for cellular function and clinical management.

| Component | Intracellular Fluid (ICF) | Extracellular Fluid (ECF) |
|-----------|---------------------------|---------------------------|
| **Major cation** | Potassium (K⁺) | Sodium (Na⁺) |
| **Major anion** | Phosphate (PO₄³⁻), proteins | Chloride (Cl⁻), bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) |
| **Sodium concentration** | Low (~10 mEq/L) | High (~142 mEq/L) |
| **Potassium concentration** | High (~140 mEq/L) | Low (~4 mEq/L) |
| **Protein concentration** | High | Low |
| **Glucose** | Variable | ~70-100 mg/dL |

**Clinical Relevance:**  
The cell membrane maintains these differences through active transport (the sodium-potassium pump). Disruption of this pump (as in cell death or hypoxia) causes sodium and water to enter the cell, leading to cellular swelling. CHEWS must understand why intravenous fluid choice matters: giving pure water intravenously would cause hemolysis (destruction of red blood cells). Giving excessive saline can cause fluid overload.

### 2.3 Electrolytes and Their Functions

**Definition:**  
Electrolytes are substances that dissociate into ions when dissolved in water, enabling them to conduct electricity. They are essential for fluid balance, nerve transmission, muscle contraction, and acid-base regulation.

**Major Electrolytes and Their Functions:**

| Electrolyte | Normal Range | Functions | Clinical Imbalance |
|-------------|--------------|-----------|----------------------|
| **Sodium (Na⁺)** | 135-145 mEq/L | Maintains ECF volume, nerve impulse transmission, muscle contraction, acid-base balance | **Hyponatremia:** confusion, seizures, coma. **Hypernatremia:** thirst, agitation, edema |
| **Potassium (K⁺)** | 3.5-5.0 mEq/L | Maintains ICF volume, cardiac and skeletal muscle function, nerve transmission | **Hypokalemia:** weakness, arrhythmias, ileus. **Hyperkalemia:** cardiac arrest risk |
| **Calcium (Ca²⁺)** | 8.5-10.5 mg/dL | Bone formation, blood clotting, muscle contraction, nerve function | **Hypocalcemia:** tetany, seizures. **Hypercalcemia:** kidney stones, confusion |
| **Magnesium (Mg²⁺)** | 1.5-2.5 mEq/L | Enzyme activation, muscle and nerve function, protein synthesis | **Hypomagnesemia:** tremors, arrhythmias. **Hypermagnesemia:** hypotension, respiratory depression |
| **Chloride (Cl⁻)** | 95-105 mEq/L | Maintains fluid balance, forms stomach acid (HCl), acid-base balance | **Hypochloremia:** metabolic alkalosis. **Hyperchloremia:** metabolic acidosis |
| **Bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻)** | 22-26 mEq/L | Major buffer in blood, acid-base balance | **Low:** metabolic acidosis. **High:** metabolic alkalosis |
| **Phosphate (PO₄³⁻)** | 2.5-4.5 mg/dL | Bone formation, ATP production, intracellular energy, acid-base balance | **Hypophosphatemia:** weakness, respiratory failure. **Hyperphosphatemia:** hypocalcemia |

### 2.4 Diffusion, Osmosis, Filtration, and Active Transport

**Diffusion:**  
The movement of molecules from an area of higher concentration to an area of lower concentration until equilibrium is reached. It does not require energy. Examples include the movement of oxygen from the lungs into the blood, and carbon dioxide from blood into the lungs.
- **Simple Diffusion:** Small, nonpolar molecules (O₂, CO₂) pass directly through the lipid bilayer.
- **Facilitated Diffusion:** Larger or polar molecules (glucose, amino acids) move through protein channels or carriers. Still passive—does not require ATP.

**Osmosis:**  
The diffusion of water across a selectively permeable membrane from an area of lower solute concentration (higher water concentration) to an area of higher solute concentration (lower water concentration). Osmosis is critical for maintaining cell volume.
- **Tonicity Terms:**
  - **Isotonic Solution:** Same solute concentration as the cell. No net water movement. Normal saline (0.9% NaCl) is isotonic.
  - **Hypotonic Solution:** Lower solute concentration than the cell. Water moves into the cell, causing it to swell and possibly burst (lysis).
  - **Hypertonic Solution:** Higher solute concentration than the cell. Water moves out of the cell, causing it to shrink (crenation).

**Clinical Relevance:**  
Intravenous fluids must be isotonic to prevent hemolysis or crenation of red blood cells. Oral rehydration solution (ORS) must be formulated correctly so it is slightly hypotonic to promote absorption while not causing dangerous shifts.

**Filtration:**  
The movement of water and dissolved substances across a membrane from an area of high pressure to an area of low pressure. Filtration is driven by hydrostatic pressure. Examples include:
- Blood pressure forcing fluid out of capillaries into interstitial spaces.
- The formation of urine in the kidneys (glomerular filtration).

**Active Transport:**  
Movement of substances against their concentration gradient (from low to high concentration). Requires energy in the form of ATP and carrier proteins.

**The Sodium-Potassium Pump (Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase):**
This is the most important active transport mechanism in the body. It pumps three sodium ions (Na⁺) out of the cell and two potassium ions (K⁺) into the cell, using ATP as energy.
- **Functions:**
  - Maintains the electrochemical gradient essential for nerve impulse transmission.
  - Maintains cell volume by keeping sodium out; otherwise, water would follow by osmosis and the cell would swell.
  - Provides the driving force for secondary active transport (e.g., glucose absorption in the intestine).
- **Clinical Relevance:**  
Drugs called cardiac glycosides (e.g., digoxin) inhibit the Na⁺/K⁺ pump in heart muscle cells, increasing calcium availability and strengthening heart contractions. Understanding this helps CHEWS appreciate drug actions.

**Secondary Active Transport (Cotransport):**
Uses the energy stored in the sodium gradient (created by the Na⁺/K⁺ pump) to move other substances against their gradient. Example: Glucose and amino acid absorption in the small intestine.

### 2.5 Acid-Base Balance

The pH of body fluids must be maintained within a narrow range (arterial blood pH 7.35–7.45) for enzymes and cellular functions to operate normally.

**pH Scale:**
- pH < 7.35: Acidosis
- pH 7.35–7.45: Normal
- pH > 7.45: Alkalosis

**Buffers:**  
Buffers are chemical systems that resist changes in pH by accepting or donating hydrogen ions (H⁺). The major buffer systems are:
1. **Bicarbonate Buffer System:** The most important buffer in blood. Carbonic acid (H₂CO₃) and bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) act as a pair.
2. **Phosphate Buffer System:** Important in the kidneys and intracellular fluid.
3. **Protein Buffer System:** Hemoglobin in red blood cells and plasma proteins are important buffers.

**Respiratory Regulation:**
The lungs regulate pH by controlling carbon dioxide (CO₂) exhalation.
- When pH falls (acidosis), breathing rate increases to blow off CO₂, reducing carbonic acid.
- When pH rises (alkalosis), breathing rate decreases to retain CO₂.

**Renal Regulation:**
The kidneys regulate pH by excreting hydrogen ions (H⁺) and reabsorbing or generating bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻). This is slower but more powerful than respiratory regulation.

**Acid-Base Imbalances:**

| Condition | pH | Primary Problem | Common Causes |
|-----------|----|-----------------|---------------|
| **Metabolic Acidosis** | < 7.35 | ↓ HCO₃⁻ | Severe diarrhea, diabetic ketoacidosis, kidney failure, starvation |
| **Metabolic Alkalosis** | > 7.45 | ↑ HCO₃⁻ | Vomiting, antacid overdose, dehydration |
| **Respiratory Acidosis** | < 7.35 | ↑ CO₂ | COPD, pneumonia, chest injury, opioid overdose (slow breathing) |
| **Respiratory Alkalosis** | > 7.45 | ↓ CO₂ | Hyperventilation (anxiety, high altitude, fever) |

**Clinical Relevance for CHEWS:**  
Severe diarrhea causes metabolic acidosis through loss of bicarbonate. Vomiting causes metabolic alkalosis through loss of stomach acid. Rapid breathing in a feverish child can cause respiratory alkalosis. Recognizing these helps CHEWS provide appropriate first-line care and referral.

### 2.6 Fluid and Electrolyte Imbalance

**Dehydration:**  
Dehydration occurs when fluid output exceeds fluid intake. It is classified by the type of fluid lost:
- **Isotonic Dehydration:** Equal loss of water and electrolytes (e.g., hemorrhage, severe vomiting/diarrhea).
- **Hypotonic Dehydration:** More salt lost than water (e.g., sweating with only water replacement).
- **Hypertonic Dehydration:** More water lost than salt (e.g., fever, diabetes insipidus).

**Signs and Symptoms of Dehydration:**
- Mild (3-5% loss): Thirst, dry mouth, slightly decreased urine.
- Moderate (6-9% loss): Sunken eyes, decreased skin turgor, dry mucous membranes, tachycardia, irritability, oliguria.
- Severe (>10% loss): Hypotension, rapid weak pulse, confusion, anuria, shock, death.

**Management:**
- **Mild to Moderate:** Oral Rehydration Therapy (ORT) using WHO-recommended ORS.
- **Severe:** Intravenous fluid resuscitation and urgent referral.

**Edema:**  
Edema is the accumulation of excess fluid in the interstitial spaces, causing swelling. It results from:
- Increased capillary hydrostatic pressure (e.g., heart failure, venous obstruction).
- Decreased plasma protein concentration (e.g., malnutrition, liver disease, nephrotic syndrome).
- Increased capillary permeability (e.g., inflammation, burns, allergic reactions).
- Lymphatic obstruction (e.g., filariasis, surgical removal of lymph nodes).

**Clinical Relevance:**  
Edema in the legs may indicate heart failure or kidney disease. Facial and periorbital edema may indicate kidney disease. Abdominal edema (ascites) may indicate liver failure or severe malnutrition. CHEWS should recognize these signs and refer appropriately.

**IV Fluid Therapy (Brief Overview):**
- **Normal Saline (0.9% NaCl):** Isotonic; used for volume resuscitation.
- **Dextrose Saline (D5W):** Initially isotonic, but dextrose is metabolized, leaving free water. Used for maintenance and hydration.
- **Ringer's Lactate:** Isotonic; contains lactate, which is converted to bicarbonate in the liver, making it useful for correcting metabolic acidosis.
- **Oral Rehydration Salts (ORS):** WHO formula contains sodium chloride, potassium chloride, trisodium citrate, and glucose. Glucose enhances sodium absorption via cotransport.

## MODULE 3: CELL BIOLOGY

### Overview
Cells are the fundamental units of life. Every function of the human body—whether it is contracting a muscle, transmitting a nerve impulse, or secreting a hormone—occurs at the cellular level. CHEWS must understand cell biology because disease begins with cellular dysfunction: infection by pathogens targets cells, cancer arises from abnormal cell division, and genetic disorders stem from DNA mutations.

### 3.1 Cell Structure

Most human cells share common structural features, though they vary in size, shape, and specialized functions.

**The Plasma Membrane (Cell Membrane):**
The plasma membrane is the outer boundary of the cell. It separates the intracellular environment from the extracellular environment and regulates what enters and leaves the cell.
- **Structure:** Described by the fluid mosaic model, the membrane consists of a double layer (bilayer) of phospholipids with proteins embedded within it. Carbohydrate chains are attached to some proteins and lipids on the outer surface, forming glycoproteins and glycolipids.
- **Phospholipids:** Each molecule has a hydrophilic (water-loving) phosphate head facing outward and two hydrophobic (water-fearing) fatty acid tails facing inward.
- **Membrane Proteins:**
  - **Integral Proteins:** Span the membrane; act as channels, carriers, or receptors.
  - **Peripheral Proteins:** Attached to the inner or outer surface; often enzymes or structural anchors.
- **Functions:**
  - Physical barrier and protection.
  - Selective permeability (regulating transport).
  - Cell recognition and communication (glycoproteins act as identification markers).
  - Signal transduction (receptors bind hormones and neurotransmitters).

**The Cytoplasm:**
Cytoplasm is the material between the plasma membrane and the nucleus. It consists of:
- **Cytosol:** The fluid portion; a jelly-like substance containing water, ions, nutrients, and dissolved gases.
- **Organelles:** Specialized structures with specific functions.
- **Inclusions:** Non-living, temporary storage substances such as glycogen granules and lipid droplets.

**The Nucleus:**
The control center of the cell, containing the genetic material (DNA).
- **Nuclear Envelope:** A double membrane with nuclear pores that regulate the passage of substances between nucleus and cytoplasm.
- **Nucleolus:** Dense region where ribosomal RNA (rRNA) is synthesized and ribosomal subunits are assembled.
- **Chromatin:** DNA and protein complex. During cell division, chromatin condenses into visible chromosomes.

### 3.2 Organelles and Their Functions

| Organelle | Structure | Function |
|-----------|-----------|----------|
| **Mitochondria** | Double-membraned with inner folds (cristae) | "Powerhouse of the cell"; produces ATP via cellular respiration. Contains its own DNA. |
| **Ribosomes** | Small颗粒 made of rRNA and protein | Protein synthesis; read mRNA and assemble amino acids into polypeptides. Free in cytosol or bound to rough ER. |
| **Rough Endoplasmic Reticulum (Rough ER)** | Membranous network with ribosomes attached | Synthesis and modification of proteins destined for secretion or membrane insertion. |
| **Smooth Endoplasmic Reticulum (Smooth ER)** | Membranous network without ribosomes | Lipid synthesis, detoxification of drugs and poisons, calcium storage. |
| **Golgi Apparatus (Golgi Complex)** | Stacked flattened membranous sacs (cisternae) | Modifies, packages, and sorts proteins and lipids into vesicles for transport. |

### 3.3 DNA and Chromosomes

**DNA (Deoxyribonucleic Acid):**
DNA is the hereditary molecule that carries instructions for the development, functioning, growth, and reproduction of all living organisms. It is a double helix composed of nucleotides.
- **Nucleotide Structure:** Each nucleotide contains a sugar (deoxyribose), a phosphate group, and a nitrogenous base.
- **Nitrogenous Bases:** Adenine (A), Thymine (T), Cytosine (C), Guanine (G).
- **Base Pairing:** A pairs with T (two hydrogen bonds), and C pairs with G (three hydrogen bonds).
- **Gene:** A segment of DNA that codes for a specific protein. The human genome contains approximately 20,000-25,000 genes.

**Chromosomes:**
- In human somatic (body) cells, DNA is organized into 46 chromosomes (23 pairs).
- **Homologous Pairs:** One chromosome from each pair is inherited from the mother, and one from the father.
- **Sex Chromosomes:** The 23rd pair determines sex (XX for female, XY for male).
- **Chromosome Structure:** Each chromosome consists of two sister chromatids joined at the centromere during cell division.

### 3.4 Protein Synthesis

Protein synthesis is the process by which cells build proteins based on instructions encoded in DNA. It occurs in two main stages: transcription and translation.

**Transcription (in the nucleus):**
- The DNA double helix unwinds at the gene region.
- One DNA strand serves as a template.
- RNA polymerase synthesizes a complementary messenger RNA (mRNA) strand.
- mRNA processing: Introns (non-coding segments) are removed; exons (coding segments) are spliced together. A 5' cap and poly-A tail are added.
- The mature mRNA exits the nucleus through nuclear pores.

**Translation (in the cytoplasm, at ribosomes):**
- mRNA binds to a ribosome.
- Transfer RNA (tRNA) molecules bring specific amino acids to the ribosome.
- Each tRNA has an anticodon that pairs with the corresponding codon on mRNA.
- The ribosome catalyzes peptide bond formation between amino acids, creating a polypeptide chain.
- Translation continues until a stop codon is reached.
- The polypeptide folds into its functional three-dimensional protein structure.

**Clinical Relevance:**  
Many genetic diseases result from errors in protein synthesis. Sickle cell anemia, for example, is caused by a single nucleotide mutation in the beta-globin gene, producing abnormal hemoglobin. Understanding protein synthesis helps CHEWS appreciate how genetic disorders arise and why some drugs target ribosomal function (e.g., antibiotics).

### 3.5 ATP Production

Adenosine Triphosphate (ATP) is the primary energy currency of the cell. Cells require a constant supply of ATP to perform work.

**ATP Structure:**
ATP consists of adenine (a nitrogenous base), ribose (a sugar), and three phosphate groups. Energy is stored in the high-energy bonds between phosphate groups. When the terminal phosphate is hydrolyzed, ATP becomes ADP (adenosine diphosphate) + Pi (inorganic phosphate) + energy.

**Cellular Respiration:**
ATP is produced mainly in the mitochondria through cellular respiration, which requires glucose and oxygen.

**Stages of Cellular Respiration:**
1. **Glycolysis (in the cytoplasm):** One glucose molecule (6 carbons) is broken down into two pyruvate molecules (3 carbons each). Net gain: 2 ATP and 2 NADH.
2. **Krebs Cycle (Citric Acid Cycle) (in mitochondrial matrix):** Pyruvate is converted to acetyl-CoA and enters the cycle. Each glucose yields 2 ATP, 6 NADH, and 2 FADH₂.
3. **Electron Transport Chain (ETC) (on inner mitochondrial membrane):** NADH and FADH₂ donate electrons, which pass through protein complexes, pumping protons to create a gradient. Oxygen is the final electron acceptor, forming water. ATP synthase uses the proton gradient to produce ATP. Yield: approximately 26-28 ATP.

**Total ATP per Glucose:** Approximately 30-32 ATP molecules.

**Anaerobic Respiration (Fermentation):**
When oxygen is insufficient, cells can produce ATP without oxygen through glycolysis followed by fermentation. This yields only 2 ATP per glucose and produces lactic acid (in humans) or ethanol and CO₂ (in yeast). Anaerobic respiration is important during intense exercise but is inefficient for sustained activity.

**Clinical Relevance:**  
Cells with high energy demands (heart muscle, neurons) are rich in mitochondria and highly dependent on oxygen. This explains why heart attacks (myocardial infarction) and strokes (cerebrovascular accidents) cause rapid cell death—the affected cells are deprived of oxygen and cannot produce sufficient ATP.

### 3.6 Cell Division

Cell division is essential for growth, repair, and reproduction. There are two types: mitosis and meiosis.

**The Cell Cycle:**
The cell cycle describes the series of events through which a cell passes between one division and the next.

| Phase | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| **G₁ (Gap 1)** | Cell growth, normal metabolic activity, protein synthesis. The cell prepares for DNA replication. |
| **S (Synthesis)** | DNA replication occurs. Each chromosome is duplicated into two sister chromatids. |
| **G₂ (Gap 2)** | Further growth and synthesis of proteins needed for cell division. |
| **M (Mitosis)** | Nuclear and cytoplasmic division. |
| **G₀ (Quiescent)** | Non-dividing state. Some cells (neurons, cardiac muscle) remain here permanently. |

**Checkpoints:** The cell cycle is regulated at checkpoints (G₁/S, G₂/M, and spindle checkpoint) to ensure damaged DNA is not replicated and chromosomes are properly aligned. These are controlled by cyclins and cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs).

### 3.7 Mitosis and Meiosis

**Mitosis:**
Mitosis produces two genetically identical daughter cells, each with 46 chromosomes (diploid, 2n). It is used for growth, tissue repair, and asexual reproduction.

**Stages of Mitosis:**
1. **Prophase:** Chromatin condenses into visible chromosomes. The nuclear envelope breaks down. Centrioles migrate to opposite poles, and spindle fibers form.
2. **Metaphase:** Chromosomes align at the metaphase plate (cell equator). Spindle fibers attach to centromeres.
3. **Anaphase:** Sister chromatids separate and are pulled toward opposite poles. Each chromatid is now an independent chromosome.
4. **Telophase:** Chromosomes arrive at poles and decondense. Nuclear envelopes reform. Cytokinesis (cytoplasmic division) begins, producing two separate cells.

**Meiosis:**
Meiosis produces four genetically diverse daughter cells, each with 23 chromosomes (haploid, n). It occurs only in gamete-producing cells (ovaries and testes) and is essential for sexual reproduction.

**Stages of Meiosis:**
- **Meiosis I (Reductional Division):** Homologous chromosomes pair up, exchange genetic material (crossing over), and separate. The chromosome number is halved from 2n to n.
  - Prophase I, Metaphase I, Anaphase I, Telophase I.
- **Meiosis II (Equational Division):** Similar to mitosis; sister chromatids separate. Produces four haploid cells.
  - Prophase II, Metaphase II, Anaphase II, Telophase II.

**Significance of Meiosis:**
- Halves chromosome number so that fertilization restores diploid number.
- Crossing over and random assortment create genetic diversity.

### 3.8 Mutation and Cancer

**Mutation:**
A mutation is a permanent change in the DNA sequence. Mutations can be spontaneous (errors during DNA replication) or induced by mutagens.

**Causes of Mutations:**
- **Chemical Mutagens:** Cigarette smoke, asbestos, benzene, aflatoxins (found in improperly stored grains).
- **Physical Mutagens:** Ultraviolet (UV) radiation, ionizing radiation (X-rays, gamma rays).
- **Biological Mutagens:** Viruses (e.g., HPV causes cervical cancer; Hepatitis B virus causes liver cancer).
- **Spontaneous Errors:** DNA polymerase occasionally makes mistakes during replication.

**Types of Mutations:**
- **Point Mutation:** Change in a single nucleotide base.
  - **Silent Mutation:** No change in amino acid.
  - **Missense Mutation:** Different amino acid produced.
  - **Nonsense Mutation:** Produces a stop codon prematurely; usually devastating.
- **Chromosomal Mutation:** Large-scale changes such as deletions, duplications, inversions, and translocations.

**Genetic Disorders (Clinical Correlations):**
- **Sickle Cell Anemia:** Point mutation in the HBB gene; glutamic acid is replaced by valine at position 6 of the beta-globin chain. Red blood cells become sickle-shaped, causing occlusion of small vessels, pain crises, and hemolysis.
- **Cystic Fibrosis:** Mutation in the CFTR gene; affects chloride transport, leading to thick mucus in lungs and pancreas.
- **Down Syndrome (Trisomy 21):** Nondisjunction during meiosis produces an extra chromosome 21. Causes intellectual disability, characteristic facial features, and heart defects.

**Cancer Development (Oncogenesis):**
Cancer is uncontrolled cell division resulting from mutations in genes that regulate the cell cycle.

**Key Genes Involved:**
- **Proto-oncogenes:** Normal genes that stimulate cell growth and division. When mutated, they become **oncogenes**, driving excessive proliferation.
- **Tumor Suppressor Genes:** Normal genes that inhibit cell division or promote DNA repair. When inactivated, cells lose brakes on division. Examples: p53 ("guardian of the genome"), BRCA1, BRCA2.

**Characteristics of Cancer Cells:**
- Uncontrolled proliferation.
- Loss of contact inhibition (they pile up).
- Ability to invade surrounding tissues (invasiveness).
- Ability to spread to distant sites via blood or lymph (metastasis).
- Angiogenesis (stimulating new blood vessel formation to supply nutrients).
- Immortality (activation of telomerase prevents shortening of telomeres).

**Clinical Relevance for CHEWS:**
- CHEWS play a vital role in cancer prevention through health education: tobacco cessation, safe sex (HPV vaccination), dietary advice (reducing aflatoxin exposure), and promoting screening (breast self-examination, cervical cancer screening).
- Early recognition of warning signs: unexplained weight loss, persistent lumps, abnormal bleeding, non-healing sores, change in bowel or bladder habits.

## MODULE 4: TISSUES

### Overview
Tissues are groups of similar cells that work together to perform a specific function. There are four primary tissue types in the human body: epithelial, connective, muscle, and nervous tissue. Understanding tissues is crucial for CHEWS because disease and injury often manifest at the tissue level—wounds damage epithelial tissue, burns destroy connective tissue and skin, fractures break bone tissue, and paralysis reflects nervous tissue damage.

### 4.1 Epithelial Tissue

Epithelial tissue covers body surfaces, lines cavities and hollow organs, and forms glands. It has little extracellular matrix, cells are tightly packed, and it has a free (apical) surface and an attached (basal) surface.

**Functions:**
- **Protection:** Skin epithelium protects against mechanical injury, pathogens, and dehydration.
- **Absorption:** Intestinal epithelium absorbs nutrients.
- **Secretion:** Glandular epithelium produces hormones, enzymes, and mucus.
- **Excretion:** Kidney tubule epithelium excretes waste.
- **Filtration:** Kidney glomerulus filters blood.
- **Sensory Reception:** Some epithelia contain sensory nerve endings.

**Classification of Epithelial Tissue:**

**A. By Number of Cell Layers:**
- **Simple Epithelium:** Single layer of cells. Functions in absorption, secretion, and filtration.
- **Stratified Epithelium:** Two or more layers. Functions in protection.
- **Pseudostratified Epithelium:** Single layer that appears stratified because nuclei are at different levels. Found in respiratory tract.

**B. By Cell Shape:**
- **Squamous:** Flat, scale-like cells.
- **Cuboidal:** Cube-shaped cells.
- **Columnar:** Tall, column-shaped cells.
- **Transitional:** Variable shape; stretches. Found in urinary bladder.

**Types of Epithelial Tissue (Summary Table):**

| Type | Structure | Location | Function |
|------|-----------|----------|----------|
| Simple squamous | Single layer of flat cells | Alveoli of lungs, lining of blood vessels (endothelium), serous membranes (mesothelium) | Diffusion, filtration, reduces friction |
| Simple cuboidal | Single layer of cube-shaped cells | Kidney tubules, ducts of small glands, surface of ovary | Secretion, absorption |
| Simple columnar | Single layer of tall cells with nuclei near base | Digestive tract (stomach, intestines), uterus | Absorption, secretion of mucus and enzymes |
| Pseudostratified ciliated columnar | Single layer with cilia and goblet cells; appears stratified | Trachea, bronchi, nasal cavity | Secretes and moves mucus (mucociliary escalator) |
| Stratified squamous (keratinized) | Many layers; surface cells dead and keratinized | Epidermis of skin | Protection against abrasion, dehydration, pathogens |
| Stratified squamous (non-keratinized) | Many layers; surface cells alive and moist | Mouth, esophagus, vagina | Protection against abrasion |
| Stratified cuboidal | Two or more layers of cuboidal cells | Ducts of sweat glands, mammary glands | Protection, secretion |
| Stratified columnar | Several layers; surface cells columnar | Rare; male urethra, pharynx | Protection, secretion |
| Transitional | Stratified; cells change from cuboidal to squamous when stretched | Urinary bladder, ureters, part of urethra | Allows stretching as bladder fills |

**Glandular Epithelium:**
- **Endocrine Glands:** Ductless; secrete hormones directly into the bloodstream (e.g., thyroid, adrenal).
- **Exocrine Glands:** Have ducts; secrete substances onto epithelial surfaces (e.g., sweat glands, salivary glands, pancreas).

### 4.2 Connective Tissue

Connective tissue is the most abundant and widely distributed tissue type. Unlike epithelial tissue, connective tissue has relatively few cells and a large amount of extracellular matrix (ground substance and fibers). It connects, supports, protects, and transports.

**Components of Connective Tissue:**
- **Cells:** Fibroblasts, adipocytes, mast cells, macrophages, chondrocytes, osteocytes, blood cells.
- **Ground Substance:** Gel-like material filling spaces between cells and fibers. Contains water, ions, nutrients, and large polysaccharides (hyaluronic acid, chondroitin sulfate).
- **Fibers:**
  - **Collagen Fibers:** Strong, flexible, resist stretching. Found in tendons, ligaments, and dermis.
  - **Elastic Fibers:** Allow tissues to stretch and recoil. Found in skin, blood vessels, and lungs.
  - **Reticular Fibers:** Fine, branching fibers that form supportive networks. Found in lymphoid organs and basement membranes.

**Classification of Connective Tissue:**

**A. Connective Tissue Proper (Has soft matrix):**

| Type | Cells / Matrix | Location | Function |
|------|---------------|----------|----------|
| Areolar (loose) | Fibroblasts, collagen, elastic fibers; gel-like ground substance | Under epithelia, around organs, between muscles | Binds tissues, allows passage of nutrients, defense |
| Adipose | Adipocytes (fat cells) | Subcutaneous tissue, around kidneys and heart, breast | Energy storage, insulation, cushioning, protection |
| Reticular | Reticular fibers, fibroblasts, leukocytes | Lymph nodes, spleen, bone marrow | Forms soft internal skeleton (stroma) supporting blood cells |
| Dense Regular | Parallel collagen fibers, fibroblasts | Tendons, ligaments | Withstands tension in one direction |
| Dense Irregular | Randomly arranged collagen fibers, fibroblasts | Dermis of skin, organ capsules, periosteum | Withstands tension in multiple directions |
| Elastic | Elastic fibers, collagen | Walls of large arteries, ligaments of vertebral column, lungs | Allows stretching and recoil |

**B. Supportive Connective Tissue (Hard or firm matrix):**

| Type | Cells / Matrix | Location | Function |
|------|---------------|----------|----------|
| Hyaline Cartilage | Chondrocytes in lacunae; glassy matrix | Ends of long bones, nose, trachea, ribs-to-sternum | Smooth surfaces for movement, flexibility, support |
| Elastic Cartilage | Chondrocytes; many elastic fibers | External ear, epiglottis | Maintains shape while allowing flexibility |
| Fibrocartilage | Chondrocytes; thick collagen fibers | Intervertebral discs, pubic symphysis, knee meniscus | Absorbs shock, resists compression |
| Bone (Osseous Tissue) | Osteocytes in lacunae; mineralized matrix (calcium phosphate) | Skeleton | Support, protection, mineral storage, blood cell formation, leverage for movement |

**C. Fluid Connective Tissue:**

| Type | Cells / Matrix | Location | Function |
|------|---------------|----------|----------|
| Blood | Erythrocytes, leukocytes, platelets; fluid matrix (plasma) | Within blood vessels | Transport of gases, nutrients, wastes, immunity, clotting |
| Lymph | Lymphocytes; fluid matrix | Lymphatic vessels | Returns fluid to blood, immune surveillance |

### 4.3 Muscle Tissue

Muscle tissue is specialized for contraction and movement. It is characterized by elongated cells called muscle fibers that contain contractile proteins (actin and myosin).

**Types of Muscle Tissue:**

| Feature | Skeletal Muscle | Cardiac Muscle | Smooth Muscle |
|---------|-----------------|----------------|---------------|
| **Striations** | Striated (banded appearance) | Striated | Non-striated |
| **Nucleus** | Multinucleated; peripheral | Usually one or two; central | Single; central |
| **Control** | Voluntary | Involuntary | Involuntary |
| **Location** | Attached to bones, tongue, diaphragm, upper esophagus | Wall of the heart | Walls of hollow organs (stomach, intestines, blood vessels, bladder, uterus) |
| **Function** | Movement, posture, heat production | Pumps blood through circulatory system | Moves substances through organs, regulates blood vessel diameter |
| **Special features** | Long cylindrical fibers; fatigue relatively easily | Intercalated discs join cells; autorhythmic; very fatigue-resistant | Spindle-shaped cells; can sustain contractions for long periods |

**Mechanism of Contraction (Brief Overview):**
All muscle types contract using sliding filaments: actin thin filaments slide past myosin thick filaments, shortening the sarcomere. This requires calcium ions (Ca²⁺) and ATP.

### 4.4 Nervous Tissue

Nervous tissue is specialized for communication, coordination, and control. It forms the brain, spinal cord, and nerves.

**Components:**
- **Neurons (Nerve Cells):** The functional units. They generate and transmit electrical signals (nerve impulses).
  - **Cell Body (Soma):** Contains the nucleus and organelles.
  - **Dendrites:** Receive signals from other neurons.
  - **Axon:** Transmits signals away from the cell body.
  - **Synapse:** Junction between neurons where communication occurs via neurotransmitters.
- **Neuroglia (Glial Cells):** Support cells that protect, nourish, and insulate neurons. Types include astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, microglia, ependymal cells, Schwann cells, and satellite cells.

**Clinical Correlation:**  
Damage to nervous tissue is often irreversible because mature neurons generally do not divide. This explains why spinal cord injuries, strokes, and neurodegenerative diseases have such serious consequences.

### 4.5 Tissue Repair and Healing

Tissue repair occurs through two processes:
- **Regeneration:** Replacement of damaged tissue with the same type of cells. Occurs in epithelial tissue, bone, and liver.
- **Fibrosis (Scar Formation):** Replacement with dense connective tissue (collagen). Occurs in cardiac muscle, skeletal muscle, and nervous tissue.

**Stages of Tissue Repair:**
1. **Inflammation:** Blood vessels dilate, capillaries become leaky, and white blood cells migrate to the area. Redness, heat, swelling, and pain result.
2. **Organization:** Blood clot forms a scaffold; granulation tissue (new capillaries and fibroblasts) fills the wound.
3. **Regeneration and Fibrosis:** Epithelial cells regenerate over the surface; fibroblasts deposit collagen to strengthen the wound.

**Factors Affecting Healing:**
- Adequate blood supply, nutrition (protein, vitamin C, zinc), absence of infection, age, and underlying diseases (diabetes impairs healing).

### 4.6 Clinical Significance of Tissue Damage

**Fractures (Bone Tissue Damage):**
- A break in the continuity of bone. Classified as closed (simple) or open (compound, where bone protrudes through skin).
- Healing involves hematoma formation, fibrocartilage callus formation, bony callus formation, and bone remodeling.
- CHEWS role: Immobilization, referral, nutritional advice (calcium and vitamin D), and fall prevention education.

**Burns (Multiple Tissue Damage):**
- **First-degree:** Epidermis only. Redness, pain, no blisters. Heals in 3-5 days.
- **Second-degree:** Epidermis and part of dermis. Blisters, severe pain, risk of infection. Heals in 2-3 weeks with scarring.
- **Third-degree:** Full thickness (epidermis, dermis, and often subcutaneous tissue). Painless (nerve endings destroyed), charred or white appearance. Requires grafting.
- **Fourth-degree:** Extends to muscle and bone.
- CHEWS role: Cool running water on burns (not ice), cover with clean cloth, fluid resuscitation for large burns, tetanus prophylaxis, and urgent referral for second-degree and above.

**Paralysis (Nervous Tissue Damage):**
- Loss of muscle function due to damage to the nervous system.
- Causes: Stroke, spinal cord injury, polio, Guillain-Barré syndrome, nerve compression.
- CHEWS role: Rehabilitation support, prevention of pressure sores, passive range-of-motion exercises, and community education on stroke risk factors.

**Muscle Injuries:**
- **Strain:** Overstretching or tearing of muscle fibers.
- **Sprain:** Tearing of ligaments (connective tissue connecting bones at joints).
- **Contusion:** Bruising from blunt trauma; blood vessels rupture within tissue.
- CHEWS role: RICE protocol (Rest, Ice, Compression, Elevation) for minor injuries, referral for severe cases, and rehabilitation advice.

## MODULE 5: GLANDULAR PHYSIOLOGY

### Overview
Glands are specialized organs or cells that synthesize and secrete substances. They are classified as exocrine (secrete through ducts to epithelial surfaces) or endocrine (secrete hormones directly into the bloodstream). This module covers both systems, with emphasis on the endocrine system because hormonal imbalances produce some of the most common conditions encountered by CHEWS—diabetes, goiter, and adrenal disorders.

### 5.1 Exocrine Glands

Exocrine glands secrete their products onto epithelial surfaces through ducts. They are essential for digestion, temperature regulation, lubrication, and protection.

**Classification of Exocrine Glands:**
- **By Structure:**
  - **Unicellular:** Single-celled glands (e.g., goblet cells in the respiratory and intestinal tracts, which secrete mucus).
  - **Multicellular:** Composed of many cells. Includes sweat glands, salivary glands, mammary glands, pancreas, and liver.
- **By Secretory Product:**
  - **Serous Glands:** Secrete watery, protein-rich fluid (e.g., parotid salivary gland, pancreas).
  - **Mucous Glands:** Secrete thick, viscous mucus (e.g., goblet cells, sublingual salivary gland).
  - **Mixed Glands:** Secrete both serous and mucus products (e.g., submandibular salivary gland).
- **By Mechanism of Secretion:**
  - **Merocrine (Eccrine):** Secretion is released by exocytosis without cellular damage (e.g., pancreas, salivary glands, eccrine sweat glands).
  - **Apocrine:** Secretion involves the pinching off of the apical portion of the cell (e.g., mammary glands, axillary sweat glands).
  - **Holocrine:** The entire cell ruptures to release its product (e.g., sebaceous glands of the skin).

**Major Exocrine Glands and Their Functions:**

| Gland | Location | Secretion | Function |
|-------|----------|-----------|----------|
| **Eccrine Sweat Glands** | Skin (widespread) | Water, salts, urea | Thermoregulation through evaporative cooling; waste excretion |
| **Apocrine Sweat Glands** | Axilla, anogenital region | Thick sweat + proteins | Scent production (activated at puberty) |
| **Sebaceous Glands** | Skin (associated with hair follicles) | Sebum (oil) | Lubricates skin and hair; antimicrobial properties |
| **Salivary Glands** | Mouth (parotid, submandibular, sublingual) | Saliva | Moistens food, begins starch digestion (amylase), antimicrobial, taste, speech |
| **Pancreas (exocrine portion)** | Abdomen | Pancreatic juice (enzymes and bicarbonate) | Digestion of carbohydrates, proteins, and fats; neutralizes stomach acid |
| **Liver** | Upper right abdomen | Bile | Emulsifies fats for digestion; excretes bilirubin |
| **Mammary Glands** | Breasts | Milk | Nutrition for infants; immune protection (contains antibodies) |
| **Lacrimal Glands** | Upper lateral aspect of each orbit | Tears | Lubricate and protect the eye surface; contain lysozyme (antibacterial) |

### 5.2 Endocrine Glands and Hormones

**Definition:**  
The endocrine system consists of ductless glands that secrete hormones—chemical messengers—directly into the bloodstream. Hormones travel to target cells or organs that have specific receptors for them.

**Characteristics of Hormones:**
- Produced in small quantities but have powerful effects.
- Regulate metabolism, growth, development, reproduction, and behavior.
- Act slowly but have prolonged effects compared to nerve impulses.
- Regulated primarily by negative feedback.

**Mechanisms of Hormone Action:**
1. **Steroid Hormones:** Derived from cholesterol; lipid-soluble. They pass through the plasma membrane and bind to intracellular receptors, directly affecting gene transcription. Examples: cortisol, estrogen, testosterone, aldosterone.
2. **Non-Steroid (Peptide and Amino Acid-Derived) Hormones:** Water-soluble; cannot cross the cell membrane. They bind to receptors on the cell surface, activating second messenger systems (e.g., cAMP, IP₃/DAG cascade). Examples: insulin, glucagon, adrenaline, thyroid hormones (though thyroid hormones are an exception and enter cells).

### 5.3 Major Endocrine Glands

**Pituitary Gland (Hypophysis):**
- Location: Small, pea-sized gland at the base of the brain, in the sella turcica of the sphenoid bone.
- Often called the "master gland" because its hormones regulate many other endocrine glands.
- Divided into:
  - **Anterior Pituitary (Adenohypophysis):** Secretes hormones regulated by the hypothalamus.
    - **Growth Hormone (GH):** Stimulates body growth, protein synthesis, and fat breakdown.
    - **Thyroid-Stimulating Hormone (TSH):** Stimulates the thyroid gland to produce thyroid hormones.
    - **Adrenocorticotropic Hormone (ACTH):** Stimulates the adrenal cortex to release cortisol.
    - **Follicle-Stimulating Hormone (FSH):** Stimulates gamete production (sperm and egg development).
    - **Luteinizing Hormone (LH):** Triggers ovulation and stimulates testosterone production.
    - **Prolactin (PRL):** Stimulates milk production.
  - **Posterior Pituitary (Neurohypophysis):** Stores and releases hormones produced by the hypothalamus.
    - **Antidiuretic Hormone (ADH / Vasopressin):** Promotes water reabsorption in the kidneys, reducing urine output. Also constricts blood vessels.
    - **Oxytocin:** Stimulates uterine contractions during childbirth and milk ejection during breastfeeding.

**Thyroid Gland:**
- Location: Anterior neck, below the thyroid cartilage (Adam's apple), consisting of two lobes connected by an isthmus.
- Produces:
  - **Thyroxine (T₄) and Triiodothyronine (T₃):** Regulate metabolic rate, body temperature, heart rate, and growth. They increase the basal metabolic rate (BMR) and are essential for normal development of the nervous system in infants.
  - **Calcitonin:** Lowers blood calcium levels by inhibiting bone resorption and promoting calcium excretion by the kidneys.
- **Iodine Requirement:** Iodine is essential for thyroid hormone synthesis. Iodine deficiency leads to compensatory enlargement of the thyroid (goiter).

**Parathyroid Glands:**
- Four small glands located on the posterior surface of the thyroid.
- Secrete **Parathyroid Hormone (PTH)**, which raises blood calcium by:
  - Stimulating osteoclasts to break down bone and release calcium.
  - Increasing calcium absorption in the intestines.
  - Decreasing calcium excretion by the kidneys.
- PTH and calcitonin work antagonistically to maintain calcium homeostasis.

**Adrenal Glands:**
- Location: One atop each kidney ("ad" = near, "renal" = kidney). Each has two distinct regions:
- **Adrenal Cortex (Outer Layer):** Produces steroid hormones.
  - **Mineralocorticoids (Aldosterone):** Regulate sodium and potassium balance, thereby controlling blood pressure and volume.
  - **Glucocorticoids (Cortisol):** Regulate metabolism, increase blood glucose, suppress inflammation and immune response.
  - **Sex Corticoids (Androgens):** Contribute to pubic hair growth and muscle development; converted to testosterone and estrogen.
- **Adrenal Medulla (Inner Core):** Part of the sympathetic nervous system. Produces catecholamines:
  - **Epinephrine (Adrenaline):** Increases heart rate, dilates bronchi, mobilizes glucose, redirects blood to muscles and brain ("fight or flight" response).
  - **Norepinephrine (Noradrenaline):** Similar effects but more potent on blood vessel constriction.

**Pancreas:**
- Location: Posterior to the stomach, extending from the duodenum to the spleen.
- Functions as both an exocrine and endocrine gland.
- **Endocrine Function (Islets of Langerhans):**
  - **Alpha (α) cells:** Secrete glucagon, which raises blood glucose.
  - **Beta (β) cells:** Secrete insulin, which lowers blood glucose.
  - **Delta (δ) cells:** Secrete somatostatin, which inhibits both insulin and glucagon.
- Insulin promotes glucose uptake by cells, glycogen synthesis in liver and muscle, and fat storage. Glucagon stimulates glycogen breakdown and glucose release from the liver.

**Other Endocrine Glands and Tissues:**
- **Pineal Gland:** Secretes melatonin; regulates sleep-wake cycles (circadian rhythm).
- **Thymus:** Secretes thymosin and thymopoietin; important for T-cell maturation and immune function. Largest in childhood; shrinks after puberty.
- **Ovaries (female):** Produce estrogen and progesterone; regulate menstrual cycle, pregnancy, and secondary sexual characteristics.
- **Testes (male):** Produce testosterone; regulate sperm production and secondary sexual characteristics.
- **Heart:** Secretes atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP), which lowers blood pressure by promoting sodium and water excretion.
- **Kidneys:** Secrete erythropoietin (stimulates red blood cell production) and renin (regulates blood pressure).
- **Stomach and Small Intestine:** Secrete gastrointestinal hormones (gastrin, secretin, cholecystokinin) that regulate digestion.
- **Adipose Tissue:** Secretes leptin (regulates appetite and metabolism) and adiponectin.

### 5.4 Hormonal Imbalance Disorders

**Diabetes Mellitus:**
- Chronic hyperglycemia due to insulin deficiency (Type 1) or insulin resistance (Type 2).
- **Type 1:** Autoimmune destruction of beta cells; usually develops in childhood or adolescence. Patients require insulin injections.
  - **Pathophysiology:** Without insulin, glucose cannot enter cells. Cells starve despite high blood sugar. The body breaks down fats and proteins for energy, producing ketones. Excess glucose is excreted in urine (osmotic diuresis), causing dehydration.
  - **Symptoms:** Polyuria (excessive urination), polydipsia (excessive thirst), polyphagia (excessive hunger), weight loss, fatigue, blurred vision.
  - **Complications:** Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), hypoglycemia, nephropathy, retinopathy, neuropathy, cardiovascular disease, foot ulcers, gangrene.
- **Type 2:** Insulin resistance with relative insulin deficiency. Associated with obesity, sedentary lifestyle, and genetic factors. Usually develops in adults but increasingly seen in adolescents.
  - **Symptoms:** Often asymptomatic for years. May present with fatigue, recurrent infections, slow wound healing, blurred vision.
  - **Complications:** Similar to Type 1 but often presents with metabolic syndrome and cardiovascular disease.
- **CHEWS Role:**
  - Community screening for risk factors (obesity, family history).
  - Health education: diet (low sugar, high fiber), physical activity, weight management, foot care.
  - Medication adherence counseling.
  - Recognition of emergencies: hypoglycemia (shaking, sweating, confusion, loss of consciousness) and hyperglycemia/DKA (fruity breath, rapid breathing, dehydration, altered consciousness).
  - Referral for eye exams, kidney function tests, and wound care.

**Goiter and Thyroid Disorders:**
- **Goiter:** Enlargement of the thyroid gland, usually due to iodine deficiency. The gland enlarges to trap more iodine from the blood.
  - **Endemic Goiter:** Common in regions with iodine-deficient soil. Preventable by iodized salt.
  - **CHEWS Role:** Promote use of iodized salt, educate on symptoms of thyroid dysfunction, and refer patients with neck swelling.
- **Hypothyroidism:** Insufficient thyroid hormone production.
  - **Causes:** Iodine deficiency, Hashimoto's thyroiditis (autoimmune), thyroidectomy, certain medications.
  - **Symptoms:** Fatigue, weight gain, cold intolerance, dry skin, constipation, depression, bradycardia, goiter.
  - **Cretinism:** Severe hypothyroidism in infants; causes irreversible intellectual disability, stunted growth, and characteristic facial features. Prevention through newborn screening and maternal iodine supplementation is critical.
- **Hyperthyroidism:** Excessive thyroid hormone production.
  - **Causes:** Graves' disease (autoimmune), toxic nodular goiter, excess iodine intake.
  - **Symptoms:** Weight loss, heat intolerance, sweating, tachycardia, anxiety, tremor, diarrhea, exophthalmos (bulging eyes in Graves' disease).

**Adrenal Disorders:**
- **Cushing's Syndrome:** Excess cortisol.
  - **Symptoms:** Central obesity, moon face, buffalo hump, purple striae, thin skin, hyperglycemia, hypertension, muscle weakness, mood changes.
  - **Causes:** Prolonged use of corticosteroid medications, pituitary tumor (Cushing's disease), adrenal tumor.
- **Addison's Disease (Adrenal Insufficiency):** Insufficient cortisol and aldosterone.
  - **Symptoms:** Fatigue, weight loss, hypotension, hyperpigmentation of skin (due to elevated ACTH), salt craving, hypoglycemia.
  - **Adrenal Crisis:** Life-threatening episode of severe hypotension, shock, vomiting, and abdominal pain. Requires emergency steroid administration and IV fluids.
- **Pheochromocytoma:** Rare tumor of the adrenal medulla producing excess catecholamines.
  - **Symptoms:** Episodic severe hypertension, headache, sweating, palpitations, anxiety.

**Pituitary Disorders:**
- **Growth Hormone Excess (in children):** Gigantism (excessive height).
- **Growth Hormone Excess (in adults):** Acromegaly (enlargement of hands, feet, jaw, and internal organs).
- **Growth Hormone Deficiency (in children):** Pituitary dwarfism (short stature but normal proportions).
- **Diabetes Insipidus:** Deficiency of ADH from the posterior pituitary. The kidneys cannot reabsorb water, producing large volumes of dilute urine and extreme thirst. Different from diabetes mellitus (which involves glucose).
- **SIADH (Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone):** Excess ADH causes water retention, dilutional hyponatremia, and confusion.

## MODULE 6: HEMATOLOGY (BLOOD)

### Overview
Blood is the only fluid connective tissue in the body. It transports gases, nutrients, hormones, and waste; regulates pH, temperature, and fluid balance; and provides defense against infection and bleeding. Blood disorders are among the most common conditions encountered by CHEWS—anemia in pregnancy and children, malaria causing hemolysis, bleeding disorders, and infections. A thorough understanding of hematology is essential for effective community health practice.

### 6.1 Composition of Blood

Blood accounts for approximately 7-8% of total body weight (about 5 liters in an average adult male, slightly less in females). It is slightly alkaline, with a pH of 7.35–7.45.

**Blood Composition:**
- **Plasma:** 55% of blood volume.
- **Formed Elements (Blood Cells):** 45% of blood volume.
  - Erythrocytes (Red Blood Cells): ~99% of formed elements.
  - Leukocytes (White Blood Cells) and Platelets (Thrombocytes): ~1% of formed elements.

**Hematocrit (Packed Cell Volume):**  
The percentage of total blood volume occupied by red blood cells. Normal values: males 40-54%, females 37-47%. Low hematocrit indicates anemia; high hematocrit may indicate dehydration or polycythemia.

### 6.2 Plasma and Plasma Proteins

**Plasma Composition:**
- Water: ~91%
- Proteins: ~7%
- Other solutes (ions, nutrients, waste, hormones, gases): ~2%

**Plasma Proteins:**

| Protein | Source | Function | Clinical Significance |
|---------|--------|----------|----------------------|
| **Albumin** | Liver | Maintains osmotic pressure (prevents edema); transports fatty acids, bilirubin, calcium, drugs | Low albumin (hypoalbuminemia) causes edema; marker of liver and kidney disease, malnutrition |
| **Globulins (alpha, beta, gamma)** | Liver (alpha, beta); plasma cells (gamma) | Transport substances (hormones, lipids); gamma globulins are antibodies (immunoglobulins) | Low gamma globulins cause immunodeficiency; elevated in chronic infections and multiple myeloma |
| **Fibrinogen** | Liver | Blood clotting; converted to fibrin during coagulation | Elevated in inflammation; consumed in disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) |

**Other Plasma Components:**
- **Nutrients:** Glucose, amino acids, fatty acids, vitamins, minerals.
- **Gases:** Oxygen, carbon dioxide, nitrogen.
- **Waste Products:** Urea, creatinine, bilirubin, uric acid.
- **Hormones:** Insulin, cortisol, thyroid hormones, etc.
- **Electrolytes:** Sodium, potassium, calcium, chloride, bicarbonate.

### 6.3 Red Blood Cells (Erythrocytes)

**Structure:**
- Biconcave disc shape (doughnut without a hole); no nucleus in mature form.
- Diameter: ~7.5 micrometers.
- Average lifespan: 120 days.
- Contains approximately 280 million molecules of **hemoglobin** per cell.

**Hemoglobin (Hb):**
- A quaternary protein composed of four globin chains (two alpha and two beta in adults) and four heme groups.
- Each heme group contains an iron (Fe²⁺) ion at its center, which binds one molecule of oxygen.
- One hemoglobin molecule can carry four oxygen molecules.
- Normal hemoglobin concentration: males 13.5–17.5 g/dL; females 12.0–15.5 g/dL.

**Functions:**
- Transport oxygen from lungs to tissues.
- Transport about 23% of carbon dioxide from tissues to lungs (as carbaminohemoglobin).
- Buffer hydrogen ions, contributing to acid-base balance.

**Production (Erythropoiesis):**
- Occurs in red bone marrow under stimulation by **erythropoietin (EPO)**, a hormone produced by the kidneys in response to low oxygen.
- Requires iron, vitamin B₁₂, folic acid, and amino acids.
- **Iron Metabolism:** Dietary iron is absorbed in the intestine, transported in blood by transferrin, and stored in macrophages and liver as ferritin. Iron is recycled from old RBCs after they are phagocytosed.

**Destruction:**
- Aging RBCs are trapped and destroyed by macrophages in the spleen, liver, and bone marrow.
- Hemoglobin is broken down: heme is converted to bilirubin (excreted in bile), and iron is recycled. Globin chains are broken down into amino acids.

### 6.4 White Blood Cells (Leukocytes)

White blood cells are the cells of the immune system. They have nuclei, lack hemoglobin, and can move through vessel walls (diapedesis) to reach sites of infection.

**Classification:**

**A. Granulocytes (have granules in cytoplasm; lobed nuclei):**

| Cell | Nucleus | Cytoplasm | Function | Normal % |
|------|---------|-----------|----------|----------|
| **Neutrophils** | 2-5 lobes | Fine pale lilac granules | Phagocytosis of bacteria and fungi; first responders to infection | 50-70% |
| **Eosinophils** | Bilobed (often kidney-shaped) | Large red-orange granules | Combat parasitic infections; modulate allergic responses | 1-4% |
| **Basophils** | Bilobed (often S-shaped, obscured by granules) | Large dark blue-purple granules (contain histamine and heparin) | Release histamine in allergic reactions; anticoagulant (heparin) | 0.5-1% |

**B. Agranulocytes (lack visible granules; round or kidney-shaped nuclei):**

| Cell | Nucleus | Cytoplasm | Function | Normal % |
|------|---------|-----------|----------|----------|
| **Lymphocytes** | Large, round, occupying most of cell | Scant blue cytoplasm | Adaptive immunity: B cells produce antibodies; T cells destroy infected cells and regulate immunity | 20-40% |
| **Monocytes** | Kidney-shaped or horseshoe-shaped | Abundant gray-blue cytoplasm | Phagocytosis; differentiate into macrophages and dendritic cells in tissues; present antigens to lymphocytes | 2-8% |

**White Blood Cell Count:**  
Normal total WBC count: 4,500–11,000 cells per microliter.
- **Leukocytosis:** Elevated WBC count; indicates infection, inflammation, tissue damage, or leukemia.
- **Leukopenia:** Low WBC count; indicates bone marrow suppression, viral infection, autoimmune disease, or chemotherapy effects.

### 6.5 Platelets (Thrombocytes)

- Small, disc-shaped cell fragments (not true cells) derived from megakaryocytes in bone marrow.
- Normal count: 150,000–400,000 per microliter.
- Lifespan: 8-10 days.
- **Functions:**
  - Form platelet plugs at sites of vessel injury.
  - Release chemicals that promote vasoconstriction and clotting (e.g., serotonin, thromboxane A₂).
  - Provide phospholipid surface for clotting reactions.

### 6.6 Hemostasis (Blood Clotting Mechanism)

Hemostasis is the process of stopping bleeding from a damaged blood vessel. It involves three phases:

**Phase 1: Vascular Spasm**  
The damaged vessel constricts (vasoconstriction) immediately, reducing blood flow.

**Phase 2: Platelet Plug Formation**  
Platelets adhere to exposed collagen at the injury site, become activated, and release chemicals that attract more platelets. They stick together (aggregation) to form a temporary plug.

**Phase 3: Coagulation (Blood Clotting)**  
A complex cascade of reactions involving clotting factors (proteins, mostly synthesized by the liver, most requiring vitamin K). It occurs via two pathways that converge:
- **Extrinsic Pathway:** Triggered by tissue factor (thromboplastin) released from damaged tissue outside the vessel.
- **Intrinsic Pathway:** Triggered by contact with negatively charged surfaces (collagen, glass) inside the vessel.
- **Common Pathway:** Both pathways activate Factor X, which converts prothrombin to thrombin. Thrombin converts fibrinogen to fibrin. Fibrin forms a mesh that stabilizes the platelet plug into a solid clot.

**Clot Retraction and Repair:**  
The clot shrinks (retraction), pulling edges of the wound together. Platelets release growth factors that stimulate tissue repair.

**Fibrinolysis (Clot Dissolution):**  
As healing progresses, the enzyme plasmin breaks down fibrin, dissolving the clot. This prevents inappropriate clot propagation.

### 6.7 Blood Groups

Blood groups are determined by antigens (agglutinogens) on the surface of red blood cells and antibodies (agglutinins) in the plasma.

**ABO Blood Group System:**

| Blood Type | RBC Antigens | Plasma Antibodies | Can Receive From | Can Donate To |
|------------|--------------|-------------------|------------------|---------------|
| A | A | Anti-B | A, O | A, AB |
| B | B | Anti-A | B, O | B, AB |
| AB | A and B | None (universal recipient) | A, B, AB, O | AB |
| O | None (universal donor) | Anti-A and Anti-B | O | A, B, AB, O |

**Rh Blood Group System:**
- Named after the Rhesus monkey in which it was discovered.
- **Rh-positive:** Have D antigen on RBCs.
- **Rh-negative:** Lack D antigen.
- Rh-negative individuals do not naturally have anti-Rh antibodies, but they develop them after exposure to Rh-positive blood.

**Rh Incompatibility (Hemolytic Disease of the Newborn):**
- Occurs when an Rh-negative mother carries an Rh-positive fetus (inherited from the father).
- During delivery, fetal Rh-positive cells may enter the maternal circulation, sensitizing the mother's immune system.
- In subsequent pregnancies with Rh-positive fetuses, maternal anti-Rh antibodies cross the placenta and attack fetal RBCs, causing hemolysis, anemia, jaundice, and potentially stillbirth.
- **Prevention:** Administering Rh immunoglobulin (RhoGAM) to Rh-negative mothers at 28 weeks and postpartum prevents sensitization.
- **CHEWS Role:** Identify Rh-negative pregnant women during antenatal care, ensure they receive RhoGAM, and educate on the importance of hospital delivery.

### 6.8 Clinical Conditions

**Anemia:**
A condition in which the blood has insufficient hemoglobin or too few red blood cells to meet the body's oxygen demands.

| Type | Cause | Diagnostic Features | Community Relevance |
|------|-------|---------------------|---------------------|
| **Iron-deficiency anemia** | Inadequate dietary iron, chronic blood loss (menstruation, hookworm), increased demand (pregnancy, growth) | Low Hb, low MCV (microcytic), low ferritin, pale conjunctiva, koilonychia (spoon nails), pica | Most common worldwide. CHEWS role: iron supplementation, deworming, dietary counseling (leafy greens, meat, fortified foods) |
| **Vitamin B₁₂ deficiency (Pernicious anemia)** | Lack of intrinsic factor (autoimmune), vegan diet, malabsorption | Low Hb, high MCV (macrocytic), neurological symptoms (tingling, ataxia) | Requires B₁₂ injections or high-dose oral supplements |
| **Folic acid deficiency** | Poor diet, pregnancy, alcoholism, malabsorption | Low Hb, high MCV, neural tube defects in offspring | Preventable with folic acid supplementation before and during pregnancy |
| **Sickle cell anemia** | Inherited point mutation in HBB gene | Sickle-shaped RBCs, hemolysis, vaso-occlusive crises, infections, stroke | CHEWS role: genetic counseling, infection prevention, pain management support, malaria prophylaxis, newborn screening |
| **Aplastic anemia** | Bone marrow failure (autoimmune, toxins, radiation) | Pancytopenia (low RBCs, WBCs, platelets), infections, bleeding | Referral to specialized centers; blood transfusions needed |
| **Hemolytic anemia** | Autoimmune destruction, malaria, G6PD deficiency, incompatible transfusion | Jaundice, dark urine, splenomegaly, elevated reticulocytes | Identify triggers (fava beans in G6PD deficiency), treat underlying cause |

**Leukemia:**
- A group of blood cancers characterized by uncontrolled proliferation of abnormal white blood cells in the bone marrow.
- **Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (ALL):** Most common in children. Sudden onset, high WBC count with immature blast cells.
- **Chronic Myeloid Leukemia (CML):** More common in adults. Philadelphia chromosome (translocation between chromosomes 9 and 22).
- **Symptoms:** Fatigue, recurrent infections, easy bruising and bleeding, bone pain, splenomegaly, weight loss, night sweats.
- **CHEWS Role:** Recognize warning signs, facilitate referral to oncology centers, and support patients through treatment and palliative care.

**Hemophilia:**
- A group of hereditary bleeding disorders caused by deficiency of clotting factors.
- **Hemophilia A:** Deficiency of Factor VIII (most common).
- **Hemophilia B (Christmas Disease):** Deficiency of Factor IX.
- **Inheritance:** X-linked recessive; predominantly affects males. Females are carriers.
- **Symptoms:** Prolonged bleeding after minor injuries, spontaneous bleeding into joints (hemarthrosis) and muscles, easy bruising, hematuria, intracranial hemorrhage.
- **CHEWS Role:** Genetic counseling, recognizing bleeding episodes, safe delivery practices for carrier mothers, referral for factor replacement therapy.

**Septicemia (Sepsis / Bloodstream Infection):**
- A life-threatening condition caused by the body's overwhelming response to a bloodstream infection.
- Pathogens (bacteria, fungi, viruses) enter the blood, triggering massive inflammation, vasodilation, capillary leakage, and organ dysfunction.
- **Symptoms:** Fever or hypothermia, tachycardia, tachypnea, hypotension, altered mental status, reduced urine output.
- **Septic Shock:** Severe hypotension unresponsive to fluid resuscitation; high mortality.
- **CHEWS Role:** Rapid recognition of danger signs, immediate referral, infection prevention (clean delivery, hand hygiene, wound care), and community education on early care-seeking.

## MODULE 7: CARDIOVASCULAR SYSTEM

### Overview
The cardiovascular system consists of the heart and blood vessels. It transports blood to every cell, delivering oxygen and nutrients and removing waste. Cardiovascular diseases—hypertension, heart failure, stroke, and coronary artery disease—are leading causes of death globally. CHEWS must understand the normal anatomy and physiology of this system to screen, educate, and refer patients effectively.

### 7.1 Anatomy of the Heart

The heart is a hollow, muscular organ about the size of a clenched fist, located in the mediastinum between the lungs, with about two-thirds of its mass to the left of the midline.

**Pericardium:**
- The heart is enclosed in a double-walled sac called the pericardium.
- **Fibrous Pericardium:** Tough outer layer; anchors the heart in place and prevents overfilling.
- **Serous Pericardium:** Two layers (parietal and visceral/epicardium) with pericardial fluid between them, reducing friction during heartbeats.

**Heart Wall Layers:**
- **Epicardium:** Outer layer; visceral layer of serous pericardium. Contains coronary blood vessels and fat.
- **Myocardium:** Thick middle layer of cardiac muscle; responsible for contraction.
- **Endocardium:** Thin inner layer of endothelium; lines chambers and covers valves; provides a smooth surface for blood flow.

**Heart Chambers:**
The heart has four chambers:
- **Right Atrium:** Receives deoxygenated blood from the body via the superior and inferior vena cavae. Contains the fossa ovalis (remnant of fetal foramen ovale).
- **Right Ventricle:** Pumps deoxygenated blood to the lungs via the pulmonary trunk and pulmonary arteries.
- **Left Atrium:** Receives oxygenated blood from the lungs via four pulmonary veins.
- **Left Ventricle:** Pumps oxygenated blood to the entire body via the aorta. Has the thickest myocardium because it generates the highest pressure.

**Septa (Walls Separating Chambers):**
- **Interatrial Septum:** Separates the two atria.
- **Interventricular Septum:** Separates the two ventricles.

**Heart Valves:**
Valves ensure unidirectional blood flow and prevent backflow.

| Valve | Location | Structure | Prevents Backflow Into |
|-------|----------|-----------|------------------------|
| **Tricuspid Valve** | Between right atrium and right ventricle | Three cusps | Right atrium during ventricular contraction |
| **Pulmonary (Semilunar) Valve** | Between right ventricle and pulmonary trunk | Three semilunar cusps | Right ventricle during ventricular relaxation |
| **Bicuspid (Mitral) Valve** | Between left atrium and left ventricle | Two cusps | Left atrium during ventricular contraction |
| **Aortic (Semilunar) Valve** | Between left ventricle and aorta | Three semilunar cusps | Left ventricle during ventricular relaxation |

**Heart Sounds:**
- **S₁ ("lub"):** Closure of the atrioventricular (AV) valves (tricuspid and mitral) at the beginning of ventricular systole.
- **S₂ ("dub"):** Closure of the semilunar valves (aortic and pulmonary) at the end of ventricular systole.
- **Murmurs:** Abnormal sounds caused by turbulent blood flow through damaged or malformed valves.

### 7.2 Coronary Circulation

The heart muscle requires its own blood supply, delivered by the coronary arteries.

**Coronary Arteries:**
- **Right Coronary Artery (RCA):** Supplies the right atrium, most of the right ventricle, and inferior part of the left ventricle. Gives off the posterior interventricular artery.
- **Left Coronary Artery (LCA):** Short trunk that bifurcates into:
  - **Anterior Interventricular Artery (Left Anterior Descending):** Supplies anterior walls of both ventricles and most of the interventricular septum.
  - **Circumflex Artery:** Supplies the left atrium and lateral wall of the left ventricle.

**Coronary Veins:**
- Collect deoxygenated blood from the heart muscle and drain into the coronary sinus, which empties into the right atrium.

**Clinical Relevance:**  
Blockage of a coronary artery (by atherosclerotic plaque or thrombus) causes myocardial infarction (heart attack). The region supplied by the blocked artery is deprived of oxygen and dies. The left anterior descending artery is the most commonly occluded and is called the "widow maker" because it supplies a large area of the left ventricle.

### 7.3 Conduction System of the Heart

The heart generates its own electrical impulses and beats rhythmically without nervous stimulation (autorhythmicity). The conduction system coordinates contractions for efficient pumping.

**Components:**

| Structure | Location | Function |
|-----------|----------|----------|
| **Sinoatrial (SA) Node** | Posterior wall of right atrium, near superior vena cava opening | "Pacemaker of the heart." Initiates each heartbeat; fires at 60-100 beats/minute. |
| **Atrioventricular (AV) Node** | Floor of right atrium, near interatrial septum | Receives impulse from SA node; delays impulse slightly to allow atria to contract fully before ventricles. |
| **AV Bundle (Bundle of His)** | Interventricular septum | Conducts impulse from AV node toward ventricles. |
| **Bundle Branches** | Right and left sides of interventricular septum | Divide to conduct impulses to each ventricle. |
| **Purkinje Fibers** | Ventricular myocardium | Large-diameter fibers that rapidly distribute impulse throughout ventricles, causing coordinated ventricular contraction. |

**Pathway of Impulse:**  
SA node → atrial contraction → AV node (delay) → Bundle of His → bundle branches → Purkinje fibers → ventricular contraction.

**Electrocardiogram (ECG / EKG):**
The ECG records the electrical activity of the heart through electrodes placed on the skin.

| Wave / Complex | Represents |
|----------------|------------|
| **P wave** | Atrial depolarization (triggering atrial contraction) |
| **QRS complex** | Ventricular depolarization (triggering ventricular contraction); atrial repolarization is hidden within it |
| **T wave** | Ventricular repolarization (electrical recovery) |
| **PR interval** | Time for impulse to travel from SA node through AV node |
| **QT interval** | Total ventricular depolarization and repolarization time |

**Common ECG Abnormalities:**
- **Bradycardia:** Heart rate < 60 bpm.
- **Tachycardia:** Heart rate > 100 bpm.
- **Atrial Fibrillation:** Irregular, chaotic atrial activity; absent P waves; irregular ventricular response.
- **Ventricular Fibrillation:** Chaotic ventricular activity; no effective pumping; cardiac arrest unless defibrillated immediately.
- **Myocardial Infarction:** ST-segment elevation (STEMI), pathological Q waves, T-wave inversion.
- **Heart Block:** Delayed or blocked impulse at AV node; PR interval prolonged or P waves not followed by QRS complexes.

### 7.4 Cardiac Cycle

The cardiac cycle is the sequence of events in one complete heartbeat (systole and diastole). At average heart rate of 75 bpm, one cycle lasts about 0.8 seconds.

**Systole (Contraction Phase):**
- **Atrial Systole:** Atria contract, pushing blood through open AV valves into ventricles. Contributes the final 20-30% of ventricular filling ("atrial kick").
- **Ventricular Systole:** Ventricles contract. Pressure rises, AV valves close (S₁). When ventricular pressure exceeds arterial pressure, semilunar valves open, and blood is ejected into pulmonary trunk and aorta.

**Diastole (Relaxation Phase):**
- **Ventricular Diastole:** Ventricles relax, pressure falls. Semilunar valves close (S₂). AV valves open, and blood flows passively from atria into ventricles.
- **Atrial Diastole:** Atria relax and fill with blood from veins.

**Volumes:**
- **End-Diastolic Volume (EDV):** Volume in ventricles at end of diastole (~120 mL).
- **End-Systolic Volume (ESV):** Volume remaining after systole (~50 mL).
- **Stroke Volume (SV):** EDV - ESV = ~70 mL (volume pumped per beat).
- **Cardiac Output (CO):** CO = Stroke Volume × Heart Rate. At rest: ~5 liters/minute.

### 7.5 Blood Vessels

Blood vessels form a closed system of tubes that transport blood.

**Arteries:**
- Carry blood away from the heart (usually oxygenated, except pulmonary arteries).
- Thick walls with three layers: tunica intima (endothelium), tunica media (smooth muscle and elastic fibers), tunica externa (connective tissue).
- **Elastic (Conducting) Arteries:** Large arteries near heart (aorta, pulmonary trunk, carotids). Rich in elastic fibers; expand during systole and recoil during diastole to maintain continuous flow.
- **Muscular (Distributing) Arteries:** Medium-sized arteries (brachial, femoral, renal). Rich in smooth muscle; vasoconstriction and vasodilation regulate blood flow to organs.
- **Arterioles:** Smallest arteries; major regulators of blood flow and resistance.

**Capillaries:**
- Microscopic vessels where exchange occurs between blood and tissues.
- Walls are one endothelial cell thick.
- Types: continuous (most common), fenestrated (kidneys, intestines), sinusoids (liver, spleen, bone marrow).
- Blood flow through capillaries is regulated by precapillary sphincters.

**Veins:**
- Carry blood toward the heart (usually deoxygenated, except pulmonary veins).
- Thinner walls than arteries, with larger lumens.
- **Venules:** Small veins that collect blood from capillaries.
- **Veins:** Contain valves to prevent backflow. Rely on skeletal muscle contractions and respiratory movements to return blood to the heart.

**Clinical Relevance:**  
Varicose veins result from incompetent valves, allowing blood to pool. Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) is clot formation in deep veins, often in the legs. If the clot dislodges, it can cause pulmonary embolism (a life-threatening blockage of pulmonary arteries).

### 7.6 Blood Pressure

Blood pressure (BP) is the force exerted by blood against vessel walls. It is highest in arteries and lowest in veins.

**Measurement:**
- Expressed in millimeters of mercury (mmHg) as systolic over diastolic (e.g., 120/80 mmHg).
- **Systolic Pressure:** Pressure during ventricular contraction (normal < 120 mmHg).
- **Diastolic Pressure:** Pressure during ventricular relaxation (normal < 80 mmHg).

**Factors Affecting Blood Pressure:**
- Cardiac output (heart rate × stroke volume).
- Peripheral resistance (vessel diameter, blood viscosity).
- Blood volume.
- Vessel elasticity.
- Viscosity of blood.

**Regulation of Blood Pressure:**
- **Baroreceptor Reflex:** Baroreceptors in carotid sinus and aortic arch detect pressure changes and signal the medulla oblongata to adjust heart rate and vessel diameter.
- **Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System (RAAS):** Kidneys release renin when BP falls. Renin converts angiotensinogen to angiotensin I, then to angiotensin II (a potent vasoconstrictor). Angiotensin II stimulates aldosterone release, promoting sodium and water retention, increasing blood volume and pressure.
- **Antidiuretic Hormone (ADH):** Released from posterior pituitary in response to high blood osmolarity or low BP; promotes water reabsorption.
- **Atrial Natriuretic Peptide (ANP):** Released from atria in response to stretching; promotes vasodilation and sodium/water excretion, lowering BP.

### 7.7 Pulse

The pulse is the palpable pressure wave generated by ventricular systole, transmitted through arterial walls.

**Common Pulse Points:**
- **Radial pulse:** Lateral aspect of distal forearm at wrist (most commonly used).
- **Carotid pulse:** Side of neck.
- **Brachial pulse:** Medial aspect of antecubital fossa (used for blood pressure measurement).
- **Femoral pulse:** Groin.
- **Popliteal pulse:** Behind the knee.
- **Dorsalis pedis pulse:** Top of foot.
- **Apical pulse:** Heartbeat heard at apex of heart (5th intercostal space, midclavicular line).

**Pulse Characteristics to Assess:**
- Rate (beats per minute).
- Rhythm (regular or irregular).
- Volume/Amplitude (strong or weak).
- Character (bounding, thready).

**Normal Adult Pulse Rate:** 60–100 bpm.
- **Tachycardia:** > 100 bpm. Causes: exercise, fever, anemia, hypovolemia, heart failure, anxiety, hyperthyroidism.
- **Bradycardia:** < 60 bpm. Causes: athletic training, sleep, hypothyroidism, heart block, certain medications.

**Clinical Relevance for CHEWS:**  
Pulse assessment is a fundamental skill. A weak, rapid pulse may indicate shock or dehydration. An irregular pulse may indicate atrial fibrillation. Absent peripheral pulses may indicate arterial obstruction or severe hypotension.

### 7.8 Clinical Conditions

**Hypertension:**
- Sustained elevation of blood pressure ≥ 140/90 mmHg (some guidelines use 130/80).
- **Primary (Essential) Hypertension:** Accounts for 90-95% of cases; no identifiable cause. Risk factors: genetics, obesity, high salt intake, sedentary lifestyle, stress, alcohol, smoking, age.
- **Secondary Hypertension:** Caused by identifiable conditions: kidney disease, hormonal disorders (Cushing's, hyperaldosteronism), coarctation of aorta, medications (oral contraceptives, NSAIDs).
- **Pathophysiology:** Chronic high pressure damages arterial endothelium, promotes atherosclerosis, increases cardiac workload (left ventricular hypertrophy), and damages kidneys and retinas.
- **Complications:** Stroke, myocardial infarction, heart failure, kidney failure, retinopathy, aortic aneurysm.
- **CHEWS Role:**
  - Routine BP screening at every community contact.
  - Lifestyle counseling: reduce salt, increase fruits/vegetables, regular physical activity, weight loss, limit alcohol, stop smoking.
  - Medication adherence support: explain that hypertension usually requires lifelong treatment even when asymptomatic.
  - Recognize hypertensive emergencies (BP > 180/120 with symptoms such as chest pain, headache, confusion, vision changes) and refer urgently.

**Heart Failure:**
- The heart cannot pump sufficient blood to meet the body's metabolic needs.
- **Left-Sided Heart Failure:** The left ventricle cannot pump effectively, causing blood to back up into the lungs.
  - **Symptoms:** Dyspnea (shortness of breath), orthopnea (difficulty breathing when lying flat), paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea (waking breathless at night), pulmonary edema (pink frothy sputum), fatigue.
- **Right-Sided Heart Failure:** The right ventricle cannot pump effectively, causing blood to back up into the systemic veins.
  - **Symptoms:** Peripheral edema (ankle swelling), ascites (abdominal fluid), jugular venous distension, hepatomegaly (enlarged liver), weight gain.
- **CHEWS Role:** Monitor weight and edema, medication adherence, low-salt diet education, early recognition of worsening symptoms, and referral.

**Coronary Artery Disease (CAD) and Myocardial Infarction (MI):**
- CAD is narrowing of coronary arteries by atherosclerotic plaques.
- **Angina Pectoris:** Chest pain due to temporary myocardial ischemia; relieved by rest or nitroglycerin.
- **Myocardial Infarction (Heart Attack):** Prolonged ischemia causes myocardial cell death.
  - **Symptoms:** Crushing chest pain radiating to left arm, jaw, or back; sweating; nausea; shortness of breath; anxiety; sense of impending doom. Some patients (especially women and diabetics) have atypical symptoms.
  - **CHEWS Role:** Immediate recognition, aspirin administration if available, position patient comfortably, activate emergency transport, and CPR if cardiac arrest occurs.

**Stroke (Cerebrovascular Accident):**
- Interruption of blood supply to part of the brain, causing brain cell death.
- **Ischemic Stroke:** Caused by thrombus or embolus (85% of strokes).
- **Hemorrhagic Stroke:** Caused by rupture of a cerebral blood vessel (15% of strokes).
- **Symptoms (FAST mnemonic):**
  - **F**ace drooping
  - **A**rm weakness
  - **S**peech difficulty
  - **T**ime to call emergency services
- **CHEWS Role:** Rapid recognition using FAST, airway protection, positioning, blood pressure monitoring, urgent referral, and community education on risk factor reduction (hypertension control, diabetes management, smoking cessation).

**Varicose Veins and Deep Vein Thrombosis:**
- **Varicose Veins:** Dilated, tortuous superficial veins due to valve incompetence. Risk factors: pregnancy, obesity, prolonged standing, family history.
- **Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT):** Clot formation in deep veins, classically causing unilateral calf pain, swelling, warmth, and redness.
- **Pulmonary Embolism (PE):** Clot dislodges and travels to lungs, causing sudden dyspnea, chest pain, tachycardia, and potentially death.
- **CHEWS Role:** Encourage movement after surgery or long travel, compression stockings for high-risk individuals, and urgent referral for suspected DVT or PE.

## MODULE 8: LYMPHATIC SYSTEM & IMMUNITY

### Overview
The lymphatic system is a network of vessels, tissues, and organs that maintains fluid balance, absorbs dietary fats, and defends the body against infection. Immunity is the body's ability to resist disease. Together, these systems are critical for community health because infectious diseases remain leading causes of morbidity and mortality, especially in developing countries. CHEWS are on the front line of infection prevention, vaccination programs, and outbreak response.

### 8.1 Lymphatic Organs and Tissues

**Lymphatic Vessels:**
- A network of thin-walled vessels that collect excess tissue fluid (lymph) and return it to the bloodstream.
- **Lymphatic Capillaries:** Blind-ended tubes in tissues; highly permeable.
- **Lymphatic Vessels:** Contain valves to prevent backflow; resemble veins.
- **Lymphatic Trunks:** Drain large regions of the body.
- **Lymphatic Ducts:** The right lymphatic duct drains the right upper limb and right side of head and thorax into the right subclavian vein. The thoracic duct drains the rest of the body into the left subclavian vein.

**Lymph:**
- Clear fluid similar to plasma but lower in protein.
- Contains lymphocytes, macrophages, and debris.
- Transported by skeletal muscle contractions and respiratory movements.

**Lymphoid Organs:**

| Organ | Structure / Location | Function |
|-------|----------------------|----------|
| **Lymph Nodes** | Small bean-shaped structures along lymphatic vessels (cervical, axillary, inguinal, etc.) | Filter lymph; immune surveillance; contain macrophages and lymphocytes that destroy pathogens. Swollen nodes indicate infection. |
| **Spleen** | Largest lymphoid organ; left upper quadrant of abdomen | Filters blood; removes old RBCs; stores blood; site of immune response; produces lymphocytes and antibodies. |
| **Thymus** | Behind sternum, anterior to heart; largest in childhood | Maturation of T lymphocytes (T cells); secretes thymic hormones. |
| **Tonsils** | Back of throat (palatine), base of tongue (lingual), nasopharynx (pharyngeal/adenoids) | Trap and destroy pathogens entering through mouth and nose. |
| **Peyer's Patches** | Wall of small intestine | Monitor intestinal bacteria; produce lymphocytes. |
| **Appendix** | Attached to cecum | Contains lymphoid tissue; may play a role in immune function and gut flora repopulation. |
| **Mucosa-Associated Lymphatic Tissue (MALT)** | Mucosal surfaces of respiratory, gastrointestinal, urinary, and reproductive tracts | Protects surfaces exposed to external environment; produces secretory IgA. |

### 8.2 Lymph Circulation

Lymph circulation serves three primary functions:
1. **Fluid Recovery:** Returns interstitial fluid to the bloodstream, preventing edema.
2. **Immune Surveillance:** Lymph passes through lymph nodes where pathogens are detected and destroyed.
3. **Lipid Absorption:** Lymphatic vessels in the small intestine (lacteals) absorb dietary fats and fat-soluble vitamins and transport them to the bloodstream.

### 8.3 Immune System

The immune system recognizes and destroys foreign substances (antigens) such as pathogens, toxins, and abnormal body cells (cancer cells). It has two arms: innate immunity and adaptive immunity.

**Innate (Non-Specific) Immunity:**
Present at birth; provides immediate, general defense. No memory; response is the same upon re-exposure.

**Components:**

| Component | Mechanism |
|-----------|-----------|
| **Physical barriers** | Skin (keratin, acidic pH, antimicrobial peptides), mucous membranes (mucus traps pathogens), cilia (sweep mucus away) |
| **Chemical barriers** | Lysozyme in tears and saliva, stomach acid (HCl), defensins in skin and mucosa, interferons (antiviral proteins) |
| **Phagocytic cells** | Neutrophils, macrophages, dendritic cells engulf and destroy pathogens |
| **Natural Killer (NK) Cells** | Lymphocytes that destroy virus-infected cells and tumor cells without prior sensitization |
| **Inflammation** | Redness, heat, swelling, pain; vasodilation and increased permeability allow immune cells and proteins to reach infection site |
| **Fever** | Elevated body temperature inhibits pathogen growth and enhances immune response |
| **Complement system** | Plasma proteins that lyse bacteria, promote inflammation, and enhance phagocytosis (opsonization) |

**Adaptive (Specific) Immunity:**
Develops over a lifetime; targets specific antigens with precision. Has memory; response is faster and stronger upon re-exposure.

**Components:**

| Component | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Lymphocytes** | B cells and T cells (produced in bone marrow; T cells mature in thymus) |
| **Antigens** | Substances that trigger an immune response (proteins, polysaccharides on pathogen surfaces) |
| **Antibodies (Immunoglobulins)** | Proteins produced by B cells that bind to specific antigens, neutralizing them or marking them for destruction |
| **Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC)** | Cell surface proteins that display antigens to T cells |

**Types of Adaptive Immunity:**

| Feature | Cell-Mediated Immunity | Humoral (Antibody-Mediated) Immunity |
|---------|------------------------|--------------------------------------|
| **Primary cells** | T lymphocytes | B lymphocytes |
| **Target** | Intracellular pathogens (viruses, fungi, some bacteria), cancer cells, transplanted tissue | Extracellular pathogens (bacteria, toxins), free viruses |
| **Mechanism** | Cytotoxic T cells (CD8+) directly kill infected cells. Helper T cells (CD4+) coordinate the response. | B cells differentiate into plasma cells that secrete antibodies. Antibodies neutralize, agglutinate, or mark antigens for destruction. |
| **Memory** | Memory T cells | Memory B cells |

**Types of T-Cells:**
- **Helper T Cells (CD4+):** Coordinate immune response by releasing cytokines; activate B cells, cytotoxic T cells, and macrophages. HIV specifically infects and destroys CD4+ cells, causing immunodeficiency.
- **Cytotoxic T Cells (CD8+):** Directly kill infected or cancerous cells by inducing apoptosis (programmed cell death).
- **Regulatory T Cells (Tregs):** Suppress immune response to prevent autoimmunity and excessive inflammation.
- **Memory T Cells:** Long-lived cells that mount rapid response upon re-exposure.

**Types of B-Cells:**
- **Plasma Cells:** Activated B cells that secrete large quantities of antibodies.
- **Memory B Cells:** Long-lived cells for rapid secondary response.

**Antibodies (Immunoglobulins):**

| Class | Structure / Location | Function |
|-------|----------------------|----------|
| **IgG** | Monomer; most abundant in blood; crosses placenta | Long-term immunity; secondary response; neutralizes toxins; opsonization |
| **IgA** | Dimer; secretions (saliva, tears, breast milk, mucus) | Mucosal immunity; prevents pathogen attachment |
| **IgM** | Pentamer; first antibody produced during primary response | Primary response; powerful agglutination and complement activation |
| **IgE** | Monomer; bound to mast cells and basophils | Allergic reactions; defense against parasitic worms |
| **IgD** | Monomer; surface of mature B cells | B cell activation |

**Primary vs. Secondary Immune Response:**
- **Primary Response:** First exposure to an antigen. Takes 7-10 days to peak. Dominated by IgM. Memory cells are generated.
- **Secondary Response:** Re-exposure to the same antigen. Faster (1-3 days), stronger, and longer-lasting. Dominated by IgG. This is the basis of vaccination.

### 8.4 Inflammation

Inflammation is a protective response to injury, infection, or irritation. The classic signs are:
- **Rubor (Redness):** Due to vasodilation and increased blood flow.
- **Calor (Heat):** Due to increased blood flow and metabolic activity.
- **Tumor (Swelling):** Due to increased vascular permeability and fluid leakage.
- **Dolor (Pain):** Due to pressure on nerves and chemical mediators (bradykinin, prostaglandins).
- **Functio Laesa (Loss of Function):** Due to pain and tissue damage.

**Stages:**
1. **Vascular Phase:** Vasodilation and increased permeability.
2. **Cellular Phase:** Margination (leukocytes adhere to vessel walls), diapedesis (leukocytes squeeze through walls), chemotaxis (leukocytes move toward chemical signals), and phagocytosis.
3. **Resolution:** Tissue repair via regeneration or fibrosis.

**Chronic Inflammation:**  
When inflammation persists for weeks or months. Characterized by presence of macrophages, lymphocytes, and fibroblasts. Can lead to tissue destruction and fibrosis (e.g., tuberculosis, chronic hepatitis, rheumatoid arthritis).

**Clinical Relevance:**  
While inflammation is protective, excessive or chronic inflammation causes tissue damage. Anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs, corticosteroids) are used to control it.

### 8.5 Vaccination

**Principles of Vaccination:**
Vaccination (immunization) is the administration of a vaccine to stimulate the immune system to develop adaptive immunity to a pathogen without causing the disease.

**Types of Vaccines:**

| Type | Description | Examples |
|------|-------------|----------|
| **Live attenuated** | Weakened live pathogen; produces strong cellular and antibody response | Measles, mumps, rubella (MMR), yellow fever, oral typhoid, BCG |
| **Inactivated (killed)** | Whole pathogen killed by heat or chemicals; safe but may require adjuvants and boosters | Polio (IPV), hepatitis A, rabies, Japanese encephalitis |
| **Subunit / Recombinant / Protein** | Use only a piece of the pathogen (protein, polysaccharide) | Hepatitis B (recombinant), HPV, acellular pertussis, pneumococcal, meningococcal |
| **Toxoid** | Inactivated bacterial toxins | Tetanus, diphtheria |
| **Viral vector** | Use harmless virus to deliver genetic material coding for antigen | Some COVID-19 vaccines, Ebola vaccine |
| **mRNA** | Deliver genetic instructions for making a pathogen protein | Some COVID-19 vaccines |

**Herd Immunity:**
When a high percentage of a population is vaccinated, the spread of disease is limited, protecting individuals who cannot be vaccinated (e.g., infants, immunocompromised). The threshold varies by disease (e.g., ~95% for measles, ~80% for polio).

**National Immunization Programs (Relevant to CHEWS):**
CHEWS are often responsible for administering vaccines and maintaining cold chain integrity.
- **BCG:** Protects against severe forms of tuberculosis (given at birth).
- **OPV / IPV:** Protects against poliomyelitis.
- **DTP / Pentavalent:** Diphtheria, tetanus, pertussis (whooping cough), hepatitis B, Haemophilus influenzae type b.
- **Pneumococcal Vaccine:** Protects against pneumonia, meningitis, and sepsis.
- **Rotavirus Vaccine:** Protects against severe diarrheal disease.
- **Measles Vaccine:** Given at 9 months and second dose.
- **Yellow Fever Vaccine:** Given at 9 months.
- **Meningococcal Vaccine:** In some regions.
- **HPV Vaccine:** Prevents cervical cancer (given to girls aged 9-14).
- **COVID-19 Vaccines:** Part of pandemic response.

**Vaccine Safety and Contraindications:**
- **True Contraindications:** Severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis) to a previous dose or vaccine component, immunodeficiency (for live vaccines), pregnancy (for live vaccines).
- **Not Contraindications:** Mild illness, breastfeeding, previous mild reaction (fever, soreness), family history of adverse events, antibiotic therapy.
- **Adverse Events Following Immunization (AEFI):** Mild and expected (fever, injection site pain, swelling) vs. severe and rare (anaphylaxis, seizures). All serious AEFIs must be reported and investigated.

### 8.6 Common Infections and Community Health Applications

**Malaria:**
- Caused by Plasmodium parasites transmitted by female Anopheles mosquitoes.
- Parasites infect liver cells, then red blood cells, causing hemolysis, anemia, and cyclical fever.
- **CHEWS Role:** Distribution and proper use of insecticide-treated nets (ITNs), intermittent preventive treatment in pregnancy (IPTp), prompt diagnosis using RDTs, artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT), community education on breeding site elimination, and recognizing severe malaria (altered consciousness, respiratory distress, jaundice, hemoglobinuria).

**Tuberculosis (TB):**
- Caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis; primarily affects lungs but can disseminate.
- Spread via airborne droplets. Latent TB infection can reactivate.
- **CHEWS Role:** Identifying symptomatic individuals (cough > 2 weeks, hemoptysis, weight loss, night sweats, fever), sputum collection support, directly observed therapy (DOT) for medication adherence, contact tracing, and infection control education.

**HIV/AIDS:**
- Caused by Human Immunodeficiency Virus; attacks CD4+ helper T cells, causing progressive immunodeficiency.
- **CHEWS Role:** Prevention education (ABC: Abstain, Be faithful, Correct and consistent condom use), HIV counseling and testing (HCT), prevention of mother-to-child transmission (PMTCT), adherence support for antiretroviral therapy (ART), and management of opportunistic infections.

**Respiratory Tract Infections (RTIs):**
- Upper RTIs: Common cold, pharyngitis, otitis media.
- Lower RTIs: Pneumonia, bronchitis.
- **CHEWS Role:** Recognizing danger signs (fast breathing, chest indrawing, cyanosis, inability to drink), referral, promoting immunization, nutrition, and hand hygiene.

**Waterborne and Foodborne Infections:**
- Typhoid, cholera, dysentery, hepatitis A and E, helminthiasis.
- **CHEWS Role:** Water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) promotion, safe food handling, oral rehydration, sanitation improvement, and vaccination where available (cholera, typhoid).

**Soil-Transmitted Helminthiases:**
- Ascariasis, hookworm, trichuriasis, strongyloidiasis.
- Hookworm causes iron-deficiency anemia through intestinal blood loss.
- **CHEWS Role:** Mass drug administration (MDA) campaigns with albendazole or mebendazole, sanitation education, and footwear promotion.

## EXAMINATION TIPS & STUDY GUIDE

### How to Study Anatomy and Physiology Effectively

1. **Understand Rather Than Memorize:** Focus on how structures relate to functions and how systems interact. If you understand why something happens, you will remember it longer.
2. **Use Diagrams:** Draw structures and label them repeatedly. Visual memory is powerful in anatomy.
3. **Make Flashcards:** For terms, definitions, normal values, and structures. Review them daily.
4. **Teach Others:** Explaining concepts to classmates or family members reinforces your own understanding.
5. **Use Mnemonics:** Create memory aids. Example: "Some People Have fallen in love online in my dreams to entertain some mad people" for cranial nerves (not covered in this course, but create your own for other topics).
6. **Practice Past Questions:** Review MCQs and essay questions from previous examinations.
7. **Relate to Clinical Practice:** Always ask, "How will I use this knowledge as a CHEW?" Connecting theory to practice enhances retention.

### Examination Strategies

**For Multiple Choice Questions:**
- Read the question carefully before looking at the options.
- Eliminate obviously wrong answers first.
- Look for qualifiers ("always," "never," "most likely") and be cautious with absolute terms.
- If unsure, choose the most specific and physiologically accurate answer.

**For Short-Answer and Essay Questions:**
- Plan your answer before writing. Use bullet points or numbered lists for clarity.
- Define key terms where appropriate.
- Use diagrams or flowcharts if permitted—they demonstrate depth of understanding.
- Include clinical examples to show application of knowledge.
- For case studies, structure your answer: identify the condition, explain the pathophysiology, list signs/symptoms, outline management, and describe prevention.

**Time Management:**
- Allocate time proportionally to marks. Do not spend too long on one question.
- Answer the questions you know best first to build confidence.
- Leave time to review your answers at the end.

### Common Student Pitfalls to Avoid

- Confusing anatomy (structure) with physiology (function).
- Mixing up arteries and veins (remember: arteries carry blood AWAY from the heart; A for Away).
- Confusing positive and negative feedback (negative is corrective and common; positive is amplifying and rare).
- Forgetting that the pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood (exception to the rule).
- Confusing the effects of insulin (lowers glucose) and glucagon (raises glucose).

## SUMMARY NOTES

### Quick Review: Key Concepts by Module

**Module 1:** Anatomy = structure; physiology = function. Homeostasis is maintained primarily by negative feedback. Directional terms and body cavities provide the language of anatomy.

**Module 2:** Body water is divided into ICF (2/3) and ECF (1/3). Sodium dominates ECF; potassium dominates ICF. Osmosis moves water; active transport (Na⁺/K⁺ pump) maintains gradients. Acid-base balance depends on buffers, lungs, and kidneys.

**Module 3:** Cells have a plasma membrane (selective barrier), cytoplasm (organelles), and nucleus (DNA). Mitochondria produce ATP. Protein synthesis = transcription + translation. Mitosis produces identical diploid cells; meiosis produces diverse haploid gametes. Cancer arises from mutations in genes controlling cell division.

**Module 4:** Four tissue types: epithelial (covering), connective (support), muscle (contraction), nervous (communication). Structure determines function in each. Tissue repair involves inflammation, organization, and regeneration or fibrosis.

**Module 5:** Exocrine glands have ducts; endocrine glands secrete hormones into blood. Major glands: pituitary (master), thyroid (metabolism), parathyroid (calcium), adrenal (stress response, salt balance), pancreas (glucose). Diabetes mellitus = insulin deficiency or resistance. Goiter = iodine deficiency.

**Module 6:** Blood = plasma + formed elements. Hemoglobin carries oxygen. WBCs defend against infection. Platelets and clotting factors stop bleeding. ABO and Rh systems determine blood compatibility. Anemia, leukemia, hemophilia, and septicemia are key clinical conditions.

**Module 7:** Heart has four chambers and four valves. Electrical impulse starts at SA node. Cardiac cycle = systole + diastole. Arteries carry blood away; veins return blood; capillaries exchange substances. BP regulated by baroreceptors, RAAS, and hormones. Hypertension is silent but deadly; heart failure and MI are emergencies.

**Module 8:** Lymphatic system returns fluid to blood, absorbs fats, and supports immunity. Innate immunity is immediate and non-specific. Adaptive immunity is specific and has memory (T cells, B cells, antibodies). Vaccination creates immunological memory. CHEWS are essential in immunization programs and infection prevention.
